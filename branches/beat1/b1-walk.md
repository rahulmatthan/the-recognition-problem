# Branch: The Walk

## Branch prose

Meera did not smile. The sentence sat in the air the way her own sentences always did when she heard them played back — slightly too neat, slightly more confident than she'd been when she said them. *He shows love in logistics.* It was the kind of formulation she made sometimes, on a Tuesday morning, while half-listening to the kettle, and then forgot she'd made. She had not forgotten this one, exactly. She had filed it as the small private joke a person makes about a marriage to keep the marriage bearable. Now Maya had handed it back to her on a Sri Lankan beach, with the plain trust that this was useful, and what Meera felt was not relief.

"In January," Maya added, when Meera said nothing. "You were talking about your anniversary."

"Yes," Meera said. "I remember."

She did remember. That was the difficulty. She had said it knowing it was true. She had also said it knowing it was the kind of true you can live alongside for another sixteen years if you don't disturb it.

Down the beach, the man with the fishing net cast it into the shallows in a slow expanding arc, the lead weights pulling the rim into a circle so briefly perfect it felt rehearsed. He gathered the net back. He did it again. Forty years of one thing, beautifully, and the sea giving him nothing he hadn't asked for.

"Do you want me to find you somewhere quiet for dinner?" Maya said, after a moment. "Or are you going back to the room?"

"I don't know yet," Meera said. "Don't book anything."

"All right."

She stood up. She brushed the sand from her legs — that part of the canon of the gesture intact — and she turned, automatically, toward the path that led back through the garden to the bungalow, and then she didn't take it. She walked the other way instead. South along the beach, past the catamaran hulls drying in the sun, past a German couple reading on towels, past a boy with a stick drawing something in the wet sand that the next wave would erase.

She wasn't deciding anything. That was what she would think later, on the plane, when she tried to assemble what had happened into a story she could tell herself. She had simply walked, the way you walk when the alternative is to walk in the other direction, and the other direction was a man in a fresh shirt practising the apology he had already drafted in his head.

The headland came up faster than she expected. There was a path of red dirt and broken coral that climbed through coconut palms and emerged on the rocks, and from there she could see the next bay, smaller than Unawatuna, less hotel and more village. She kept walking.

By the time she reached the second beach the heat had begun to tilt. A handful of fishing boats were drawn up on the sand at angles, painted blue and turquoise and a faded chemical green, and behind them a strip of buildings — three guesthouses, a temple wall, a kiosk selling king coconut. Past the kiosk, on a corner where a side road went inland, there was a tea stall.

It was someone's front room with a counter built into the doorway. A man in a checked shirt was pouring milk tea from one steel cup to another in long thin streams, the way her grandfather used to in Madras, lengthening the pour for theatre as much as for cooling. A woman sat on a plastic stool grating coconut into a steel bowl. A small black-and-white dog slept under the bench. There were no other customers.

"*Tea?*" the man said, in English, tipping his head.

"Please."

He brought her a glass set on a saucer, milky and over-sweet, and a slice of coconut roti on a square of newspaper. She paid him in rupees that were too many. He gave her some back. The woman with the coconut said something to him in Sinhala which he answered without looking up, and the dog opened one eye, considered Meera, and shut it again.

She sat on the bench with her back against the wall and watched the bay. The tide was beginning to come in, and the boats that had been out since before dawn were beginning to come back — first one, then two together, then a third on its own — and the men hauled them up the sand with the bored, expert efficiency of people who had done one thing, beautifully, for forty years.

She did not call Maya. She did not call Vikram.

She sat. The tea got cold and she ordered another one. The woman grating coconut began to grate a second coconut. The light went from yellow to copper to something thinner. A power cut took the kiosk's strip light and nobody reacted; the woman lit a small kerosene lamp from a box of matches kept under the counter and the room arranged itself around the new flame.

What Meera was doing, she thought — though *thought* was too organised a word for it — was not avoiding Vikram. She had avoided Vikram many times over the years, in many places, with many strategies. This was something else. This was sitting with the sentence Maya had given her back, and turning it, and finding that turning it didn't change it. *He shows love in logistics.* All the way through, the same sentence. Not a problem to be solved. Not a misunderstanding to be cleared. A description of how the man she had married actually loved.

And the corollary, which she had not let herself say in January and could not avoid now: *and it isn't enough for me, and I have known this for some time, and I am going to live with it anyway.*

The corollary did not arrive as revelation. It arrived as a thing she had already been carrying, the way you sometimes notice a bag on your shoulder you put down hours ago in your head but never actually set down. The walk was not the discovery. The walk was the noticing.

She thought about Riya's recital. She thought about the kitchen renovation that had taken eight months and three fights. She thought about how Vikram, right now, was probably refreshing the flight booking page and trying to decide whether cancelling the extra night would be presumptuous or generous. He would land on generous. He would be right to. He was not, in any of this, the villain — he had never been the villain, that was part of why it was so hard.

The man at the counter said something gentle in Sinhala and the woman laughed, briefly, and Meera thought how strange it was that she could sit in a stranger's tea stall in a country she did not speak, watching a private affection between two people whose names she would never know, and feel less alone than she had felt at lunch.

The boats kept coming in. By the time the last one was up the sand it was almost dark.

She walked back the long way, along the road, because she did not want to do the headland in the dark. The road had no streetlights for the first kilometre, and then a few, set far apart, hazed with insects. A motorbike passed her, then another, then a tuk-tuk with a family of four pressed into it and a child asleep against the driver's hip. The child, half-awake, raised a hand at Meera as the tuk-tuk passed, and Meera raised one back before she had decided to, and the tuk-tuk kept going, and the small unsponsored exchange stayed with her the rest of the way to the hotel as a thing she had not meant to take and was not going to give back.

It was after ten when she reached the bungalow. The garden lights were on. The pool was lit and empty. Their room was dark. She let herself in quietly.

Vikram was asleep, or doing what passed in their marriage, lately, for asleep — the breathing too even, the body held a fraction too still. His phone was charging on the nightstand. The fresh shirt was folded over the chair. He had eaten somewhere; there was a receipt on the dresser, a single main course, no wine. He had waited up for some hours and then gone to bed, and the going to bed had been, in its own way, a kind of statement.

She did not wake him. She showered. She got into her side of the bed and lay on her back, and listened to the fan turning slowly above them like a thought that couldn't quite finish, and after a while she slept.

In the morning Vikram was up before her. When she came onto the balcony with her coffee he was already in the pool, swimming laps in a slow steady freestyle, the only person down there at that hour, the surface broken in small even arcs by his shoulders. The garden boy was raking leaves at the far end. A bulbul called from somewhere and was not answered. Meera stood at the railing with her hands around the cup and watched her husband swim, and she did not go down, and he did not look up, and the morning held them both in its equable Sri Lankan light, neither of them in any obvious distress, neither of them moving toward the other, the trip continuing on its rails toward its scheduled end.

They flew home that evening. They were polite to each other in the taxi and at the airport and on the plane. At Bangalore, Riya had a cold and Arjun had broken a cabinet door, and Vikram's mother needed her arthritis prescription renewed, and the house absorbed them back into itself with the practised efficiency of a thing that had been managing without them. Within a week they were eating dinner across from each other again, talking about logistics, which was the language they had, which was — they both now privately knew, and did not say — what they had decided to keep.

## Self-review

The branch sits on the cold side of the chapter without (I hope) becoming a different kind of chapter. Reviewers should pressure-test two places. First: Meera's walk and the tea-stall sequence is the load-bearing prose, and it needs to feel like noticing rather than deciding — if it reads as her *resolving* to leave the marriage in any form, the branch has tipped over into the clean-breakup the ledger forbids. Second: the closing pool image and the paragraph that follows it (the flight home, the week after) compress a lot. I held it short on purpose so the final note isn't *they parted* but *they continued, having seen* — but it may read as too quick. The final line ("which was — they both now privately knew, and did not say — what they had decided to keep") is the chapter's hinge into the drift; it can be cut to the previous sentence if it reads as overdetermined. Voice-wise, the tea-stall passage drifts a little long; I kept it because the absence of resolution needs a real-world long minute to land, but a tightening pass is reasonable.
