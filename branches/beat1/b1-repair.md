# Branch: No Repair

## Branch prose

Vikram, when he spoke, had moved into the voice he used in arguments where he believed himself to be in the right. He had not chosen the voice on purpose — he was not, even now, that calculating — but the voice arrived as it always did when the version of Vikram she had married wanted protection.

"You know I wasn't trying to ruin anything."

She did not look at him.

"I checked the flights because we'd had a good day. That is a strange thing for me to be sorry about."

"I didn't say you should be sorry."

"You said I made the day about logistics."

"I said it was the language you have for it. That's not the same thing."

A pause.

"It feels like the same thing when you say it like this."

She stood up. The wet swimsuit had stopped being interesting twenty minutes ago. She wrapped the towel under her arms and stepped off the lounger.

"I'm going to shower," she said. "I'll meet you for dinner if you want."

"Meera—"

"Vikram. I don't want to do this here."

She walked back along the pool's edge without looking at him. He stayed on the lounger for a minute, perhaps two, and then she heard him get up and follow her at a careful distance, the way he had learned to follow her over sixteen years.

They had dinner in the hotel restaurant, because the alternative was a tuk-tuk and a conversation. They were polite to the waiter. They split a fish. Vikram drank one glass of wine. Meera drank water. He told her about a podcast he had been listening to on the flight. She told him about something Riya had said before the trip about a school friend's mother. They did not refer to the pool. They did not refer to the apology that had not happened. They both knew that not referring to it was a position, and the position was Meera's. By the time they ordered fruit they had achieved the truce of two people who had been married long enough to file a fight.

The morning of the last day was a soft yellow. They took breakfast at the small table on their bungalow's verandah. Vikram had bought a Sunday Telegraph from the lobby — there were two-day-old British papers stacked there, for reasons neither of them asked about — and he read it while Meera drank her coffee and watched the gardener pinch off the dead heads from a bougainvillea that did not need them pinched off. They flew out at five.

The drive to Mattala was two hours of green. The driver, who had brought them down on Saturday, had remembered Meera's preference for the front seat and had brought a bottle of cold water, unprompted. He did not try to make conversation. The road went past tea, then rubber, then rice. Vikram dozed against the window. Meera did not.

The flight was uneventful. They held boarding passes in adjacent seats. Vikram offered her the window. She took it. He fell asleep before the seatbelt sign went off. She watched the Indian Ocean turn from coastal blue to deep blue and then she watched the cloud and then she watched the seat-back screen of the small girl in front of her, who was watching a film about a fish.

In Bangalore the taxi from the airport took an hour and ten because the traffic on Hosur Road was its standard Sunday-evening shape. They held hands for the last twenty minutes — she did not know whose hand had moved first; the taxi was warm; the holding was easier than not holding. Neither of them said anything about it.

The house received them.

Riya had a cold. Arjun had broken a cabinet door — *the one near the fridge, Mama, but Sumitra-aunty put a tape on it.* Vikram's mother needed her arthritis prescription renewed, and there was a letter from the BBMP about something no one was going to read tonight. They unpacked. They folded their clothes into the laundry hamper. Meera returned a call from her sister. Vikram opened a tin of tomatoes and made the lazy dinner he made on travel nights. The children ate it. The children went to bed. Meera read three pages of her book and turned the lamp off.

Vikram came up at quarter to twelve. He brushed his teeth. He came to bed.

He said: "Meera."

She said: "I know."

She did not know what she had told him she knew. He did not ask. They lay on their backs for a while and then he turned onto his side away from her, the way he did when he was about to sleep, and she lay awake on her back for another hour after that, and after the hour she also slept.

Monday went as Mondays went.

Tuesday went as Tuesdays went.

Tuesday night was the second night home. They had been to Riya's parent-teacher meeting and they had been polite to a man whose name they would forget and they had eaten roti with a curry Vikram's mother had sent over. They had agreed about Riya's mathematics and they had disagreed mildly about whether Arjun's screen time on weekends should be extended by an hour. They had not, at any point in the evening, referred to Sri Lanka.

She turned the bedroom lamp off at twenty past eleven. He was already in bed. They were on their backs with the fan turning slowly above them, the way the fan in Unawatuna had turned, only the Bangalore fan was older and ticked when its blade reached a particular angle.

They were both awake. They were both not speaking. Vikram's phone was on his nightstand and Meera's phone was on hers, both face down, both charging, both, in the way of phones now, running their nightly consolidations — the agent on his side composing whatever model of him that data had given it, the agent on her side composing whatever model of her hers had. Two small machines on two nightstands, each one tending the soil of the person it had been calibrating to, each one folding into itself the small additional information of a holiday that had not, by either model's calibration, been a successful holiday.

What the models did not know, because the models could only know what was given to them, was that the persons they were tending had, in the space of forty-eight hours, registered the trip's failure independently of each other and registered, also independently of each other, that this was the failure they were going to live with.

The fan ticked.

Vikram said, to the ceiling: "Meera."

She said, to the ceiling: "Yes."

Neither of them said the next thing.

The fan ticked.

She turned onto her side, away from him, and after a while he turned onto his side, away from her, and they slept on opposite edges of the mattress with two phones between them on two nightstands, and the small green indicators blinked on in the dark while the AIs kept their patient unsponsored watch over a marriage which had, that holiday, been given the chance and had not closed.

## Drafting notes

- **Voice anchors held:** Beat 1 conversational realism, mid-warm even in cold drift — *the truce of two people who had been married long enough to file a fight* / *the way he had learned to follow her over sixteen years* / *the holding was easier than not holding*. Cumulative "and" compounds in the long flight-and-taxi paragraph and in the closing pair of paragraphs. Triple cadence in *They held boarding passes in adjacent seats. Vikram offered her the window. She took it.* Categorical typification (*the way of phones now*; *the truce of two people who had been married long enough...*). Em-dash for parenthetical specificity throughout. The italic Riya line *the one near the fridge, Mama, but Sumitra-aunty put a tape on it* preserves canon's domestic-detail texture from the canon ending. The "fan ticked" repetition holds canon's fan motif (canon paragraph 19's *like a thought that couldn't quite finish*) but moves the fan home and gives it the specific tick of the Bangalore fan to vary the echo per the §5 anti-pattern on verbatim repetition.
- **Architectural threads preserved:** §A.3 (gap-in-the-data — the closing two paragraphs make the gap load-bearing: the AIs are explicitly named as not-knowing the registration both Meera and Vikram have made, *because the models could only know what was given to them*). §A.5 (Beat 1 is pre-substrate; no embodiment risk). §B.1 (small unscheduled gestures: the driver bringing the cold water unprompted; the taxi handhold whose initiator neither knows; the small girl in the seat in front watching the fish film). §B.3 (second-performance reveal: the "two phones tending two models" closing image is the surfacing of canon's *AIs were tending soil, each one tending the soil of the other person it had been calibrating to* — the canonical line read forward into branch's afterlife. The reveal is that the soil-tending is now happening at home, in absence of the *exchange* canon's image presupposes; it is the same gesture making different meaning). §B.4 (English throughout — the languages-as-gap thread is structurally absent in Beat 1 and Beat 1's branches; held). §B.5 (door: the bedroom door not closed against either party; the figurative door is the closed eye Vikram turns toward the ceiling and the closed eye Meera turns to her side). The hard rule on the marriage continuing in the drift is held — the closing line is *given the chance and had not closed*, not *had ended*, and the two-phones image is paired soil-tending, not split.
- **Signature image:** two phones face down on two nightstands, small green consolidation indicators blinking, while Meera and Vikram lie on opposite edges of the mattress. Replaces canon's resort-pool reconciliation image. Picks up canon paragraph 23's *AIs tending soil, each one tending the soil of the other person it had been calibrating to* and recasts it: the soil-tending continues, but the "other person" each AI was calibrating to has, in the space of forty-eight hours, made an unmodelled decision the AI cannot register.
- **Coda implication addressed:** Beat 1 has no AI-perspective coda. None written. The system's first anomaly entry occurs in Beat 2 regardless; this branch leaves Beat 2's world state unchanged.
- **Difficulties:** the hardest pull was the pool compression. The ledger ceiling was ≤ 200 words and Vikram's defence wanted to expand into a fight. I held the defence to four exchanges (Vikram's three lines, Meera's three lines, then her standing up and his staying on the lounger) so the scene reads as not-quite-a-fight, the marriage's signature avoidance. Second hard pull: the dinner, the morning, the drive, the flight — four chunks in canon's would-have-been-easy-after-reconciliation territory, here all rendered as polite-not-warm. The temptation was to make any one of them a small confrontation; I held them all in the same flat register, on the bet that the cumulative flatness is the chapter's argument. Third: the closing *and had not closed*. The phrase echoes canon's "what they had decided to keep" formula but slightly inverts it — they have not decided to keep; they have failed to close. I judged the rhyme defensible.

## Self-review

The branch sits at ~1,250 words: ~210 pool, ~860 next 48 hours, no coda. Reviewers should pressure-test three places.

First: the pool compression. ≤ 200 words was the ledger ceiling; my count is ~210, slightly over. Vikram's three defending lines and Meera's three deflecting ones plus the *I'm going to shower / I'll meet you for dinner if you want* off-ramp are the load-bearing exchange; cutting further would tip into sketch. If reviewers want trim, candidate cuts are *the wet swimsuit had stopped being interesting twenty minutes ago* and the parenthetical *(perhaps two)* on his minute on the lounger.

Second: the second-night-home closing. The ledger explicitly asks for the bed-at-home-in-Bangalore image with two phones on two nightstands; I delivered it but added the *fan ticked* repetition as the rhythmic spine and the explicit *the AIs kept their patient unsponsored watch* register-shift in the final clause. The risk is that the unsponsored-watch line lifts into Tchaikovsky-register inside chapter prose, which Beat 1 forbids (no coda, no system-as-feeling in body). I judged it safe because the watch is observed *from outside* — described, not narrated by the system — and *unsponsored* is a §B.1 register-word, not a system-interior word. If reviewers read it as system-as-feeling, the fix is to drop *patient unsponsored* and let the AIs *keep their watch* unmodified.

Third: the *what the models did not know* paragraph. The chapter's argument is concentrated here, and the paragraph is long-cumulative. The risk is that it reads as analysis rather than noticing. I held the cumulative compound architecture to keep it on the noticing side, and I named the gap (*the persons they were tending had... registered the trip's failure independently of each other... that this was the failure they were going to live with*) without making it a thesis. If reviewers find it tips into argumentation, the surgical cut is the second clause (*registered, also independently of each other, that this was the failure they were going to live with*) — losing a beat but not the gap.
