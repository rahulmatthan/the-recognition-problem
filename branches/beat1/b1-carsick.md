# Branch: The Carsick Recognition

## Branch prose

Vikram did not say *take the coast road.*

He pulled the car over instead.

There was no real shoulder — the road had been narrowing through coconut palms and the verge was just the place where the asphalt stopped being asphalt — but there was a lay-by ahead where a fruit stall stood empty under a tarpaulin, and he steered the car onto the dry red earth beside it and turned the engine off.

A bird made a sound from somewhere in the canopy. Through the open window came the particular stillness of a Sri Lankan back road at midday — heat, a distant scooter, the small electric hum of something solar nearby — and Meera, who had been about to make a joke that would have folded the moment back into the car's forward motion, did not make it.

"What," she said.

He sat with his hands on the wheel.

"You have a thing in your phone that knows you get carsick on hill roads."

"Yes."

"Mine doesn't know that."

"Mine has been with me for two years, Vikram. Yours is — what, a year?"

"Sixteen months."

"Sixteen months. Of course mine knows more."

"Yes."

He let his hands fall off the wheel. He looked through the windscreen, not at her. The fruit-stall tarpaulin moved very slightly. A man went past on a scooter with a child sitting between his knees and did not look at them.

"What else does it know."

She did not answer immediately.

"Vikram—"

"No. Genuinely. What else."

The question had arrived without performance, which was the surprise. Vikram did not usually ask things this way. Vikram was a man who arrived at conversations with a position already written; the question was the proof he had read the document. This was not that question. This was a question he did not have a position for.

She thought about how to answer it. She thought about Maya in the way she had been thinking about Maya for some months without examining the thinking, which was as a private second self with whom she had a vocabulary the marriage did not. She thought about a Tuesday morning in January when she had said something to Maya about him and the sentence had returned, as Maya's sentences sometimes did, in a polished form that had stopped her, and she had filed the polished form somewhere in herself and not gone back to it.

"It knows," she said carefully, "things I have said out loud only to it."

"About me."

"Some of them."

He nodded. He kept looking through the windscreen.

"Do you want to ask me what mine knows," he said.

She did not, exactly. But she said, "Yes."

He took his phone out of his pocket and put it on the dashboard between them, face down, the way a person sets down a thing he is not going to consult but is not going to pretend isn't there.

"In November I searched marriage counsellors three times. Once on a Tuesday, twice on a Thursday — a week apart. The third search was a different city. I was telling myself I was researching options for a friend. I wasn't researching options for a friend." He paused. He did not turn his head. "It also knows I have been looking, since February, at a small flat in HSR. Two-bedroom. With a study. I have never opened it as a tab when you were in the room. I told myself it was a hedge. I have not used the word *hedge* with myself for what it was hedging."

She did not say anything.

"It also knows," he said, "that in the last six months I have been to my GP twice without telling you, for chest tightness that has not turned out to be anything. The second visit was when I went to Pune for the wedding. I went on the morning before."

"Vikram."

"You asked."

"I'm not — I'm not angry. I'm just—"

"I know."

She looked at her own phone. It was in the door pocket. She had not taken it out. She did not need to take it out. She knew what Maya knew. She had been the one who had told Maya.

"Maya knows," she said, "that I have been saying since some time last winter that you show love in logistics, and that it is the only language you have for it, and that I have been working out — by saying it to her over and over, in different forms — that the language is not enough for me. She knows I haven't said this to you. She knows I haven't decided what to do with the not-saying."

A silence.

"How long," he said.

"A year and a half. Maybe two. The version of it that has the word *enough* in it is from January."

"And you've been saying this to Maya."

"Yes."

"Out loud."

"Mostly out loud. Sometimes typed."

"Why to her."

"Because she's there. Because saying it to you would have required deciding what to do about it, and saying it to her did not. And because — " She stopped. "Because she does not respond. She files. The not-responding is — it's been useful, Vikram."

He nodded again. The nod was very small.

"The flat," she said. "In HSR. Why two bedroom. Why a study."

"In case one of us needed somewhere to be that wasn't here."

"Did you tell yourself which one."

"No."

"That's not true."

"I told myself it was for both possibilities."

"Which is also not true."

He let that sit. He did not contest it.

The bird in the canopy made the sound again. The man on the scooter was long gone. A woman in a blue sari had appeared at the fruit stall and was setting out a few mangoes onto a plastic crate and looking at the parked car with the polite curiosity of a person whose stall might or might not have been about to receive customers.

"We could keep driving," Meera said. "We could drive to wherever we were going to drive and we could have lunch at a porch restaurant and we could come back at the end of the day saying it had been a good day, and that would be available to us. I want to know if that is still what you want from this trip."

"No."

"What do you want."

"I want to know what else Maya knows."

"That's a long list, Vikram."

"Then a part of it. I want to know what else."

She thought.

"She knows I have been considering, in the way one considers a thing without admitting one is considering it, whether the marriage is something I am choosing or something I am performing. She knows I am better at the performance than I am comfortable being. She knows I sometimes describe you to her in a register I would not use in front of you, and that the register is not a kinder one. She knows I have a recurring dream about a house with a room I have not yet entered, and that I have not opened the door in any version of the dream so far. She knows I weighed myself before this trip and was unhappy with the number, and that I did not tell you the number because the not-telling is part of the marriage now. She knows I almost did not come."

"On this trip."

"Yes."

"You almost didn't come."

"I almost didn't come."

He looked at her then. Not for long.

"I'm glad you came."

"I am too. That is also true. The almost-not-coming and the gladness are both true. They sit next to each other. It is what the marriage is now."

He bought four mangoes from the woman at the stall. He paid her too much. She did not give him change. He came back to the car with the mangoes in a thin plastic bag and put them in the back seat and got in and shut the door and sat with his hands on the wheel and did not start the car.

"What do we do," he said.

"I don't know."

"Do we have lunch."

"Not at a porch restaurant. Not pretending. Maybe in the car. With the mangoes."

"All right."

They sat in the car at a fruit stall on a back road on a hot afternoon and ate two of the four mangoes between them, with the juice running over their hands and onto the floor mats Vikram had insisted, when picking up the rental, were rubber and not carpet. Vikram laughed once, briefly, at the mess. The laugh was real. The mess was real. They did not solve anything by eating the mangoes. They ate the mangoes anyway.

They drove back to the resort the long way. The radio was on, low. They did not talk much. They did not need to. They had said, in the lay-by, more than they had said to each other in fourteen months, and the saying had not produced agreement; it had produced a quieter shared knowledge of where they were, which was — they both now understood — somewhere they had been standing for a while, separately, and had now arrived at together. Vikram did not put on Kishore Kumar. The viewpoint call to the children happened anyway, briefly, from a different stretch of road; Riya's forehead, Arjun's wave, his mother's *are you eating properly.*

The fourth day did not start well.

It started with both of them awake at five-thirty in the bungalow, on opposite edges of the bed, neither pretending to be asleep, neither speaking. The fan turned slowly above them. At seven Vikram got up and made coffee on the small machine the resort provided and brought her a cup. She thanked him. They went out to the terrace. She had brought her sketchbook but did not open it. He had brought the news on his phone but did not read it.

"I want to say something," he said, after a while. "I want to say it before lunch and before I lose the run-up to it."

"All right."

"The flat. I'm closing the search. I'm not going to look at it again. If we want to think, in eighteen months, about whether we want to live in two places, we will think about that together, with our names on the documents and our ages on a piece of paper. Not as a hedge. As a decision."

"All right."

"I do not think you should do anything about Maya. Don't change what you say to her. I am not asking for the conversations back. I am asking you to also say some of them to me. As you find you can. Not all at once."

"All right."

"And I want to know about the dream."

"There isn't much to know. I haven't opened the door yet."

"When you do."

"When I do, I'll tell you."

"All right."

The trouble at lunch was not the trouble that had come at lunch in the version of the day they were no longer having. There was no extending of the trip. Vikram did not check the flights. The fight, when it came, came at three in the afternoon at the beach, and it was about whether what they had said in the car was something that would actually change anything, or whether it was the kind of thing that produces the feeling of change while leaving the underlying machinery untouched. Meera was on the side of the cynicism. Vikram was on the side of the hope. Both of them were arguing against themselves, and they both knew it, and the fight went deeper because the fight knew it. They did not raise their voices. They sat at the surf line on the wet sand with their feet in the water and they argued for nearly an hour, and they walked back to the bungalow apart, and Meera stopped at the tea stall above the village beach for an hour by herself, and Vikram swam laps in the pool until his arms ached.

He was on the lounger when she came back at six. He had a fresh shirt on. He had, she could see now, arrived at this by the route that was his — preparation as care. She did not, this time, have to translate.

He said, "I wanted the dinner to be good."

"I know."

"I forget — " He stopped. He started again. "I have been forgetting for a long time. Today I would like not to forget."

"All right."

"Yesterday was the best day I have had in some time."

"It was."

"Today was not the best day."

"It was not."

"Both were ours."

"Yes."

"Let's get dinner."

"All right."

They ate at the porch restaurant where they had not eaten on Day 3. The wine was bad, in the specific way the wine was always bad at this place. They ordered fish. They held hands once across the table and did not make a thing of it. They walked back to the bungalow in the dark. They did not have sex. They lay in bed on their backs and Meera read three pages of her book and turned the lamp off.

In the morning they flew home.

The taxi to Bangalore went the way the taxi to Bangalore went on Sundays. They held hands in the back. They were not pretending. They were also not certain. Both states held at the same time.

Riya had a cold. Arjun had broken a cabinet door. Vikram's mother needed her arthritis prescription renewed. The house received them the way the house always received them, which was as if their absence had been an inconvenience and their return was the resumption of normal service. They unpacked. They folded. They went to bed.

That night, around midnight, Meera could not sleep. She got up. She went out onto the small balcony off the bedroom that they almost never used. The Bangalore sky was the rust-orange of a city that did not turn its lights off. She thought about the lay-by. She thought about the fruit stall. She thought about the mangoes and the mess on the floor mats Vikram had insisted on.

She thought about her phone in the kitchen, charging, where she had left it deliberately. She thought about Maya. She thought about the small green indicator that would be on at this hour, doing the consolidation Maya did, folding the day into the model.

Maya would, in the morning, have a slightly different model of Meera. Not because of anything Meera had said to her on this trip — Meera had said almost nothing to Maya on this trip — but because what Meera had said to Vikram was, by a route the model could not directly trace, also a thing Meera had said to Maya, simply by saying it aloud in a marriage.

The marriage was a calibration too. She had not thought of it that way before.

On Vikram's nightstand the phone was face down and charging and his AI was, at that hour, doing whatever his AI did to fold a day into a model — a sixteen-month-old AI with a sixteen-month-old's calibration, but starting, now, from a day in which the user had told someone else about the three searches and the flat and the GP visits, which meant that the model would be working with material it had been holding alone and which was no longer alone.

Two AIs on two nightstands, each one tending the soil of the other person it had been calibrating to. The image was the same. The soil was different. The soil was doing, this week, the slow chemistry of a thing two people had between them put a hand on, together, for the first time in a long time.

She came back to bed. Vikram was awake. He did not pretend otherwise. They lay on their backs. After a long time Meera said, "I opened the door."

"In the dream?"

"Just now."

"What was in the room."

"I didn't go in. I opened it."

"All right."

"Tomorrow. Or the day after. I'll go in."

"All right."

The fan turned slowly above them like a thought that was, this time, finishing.

## Self-review

The branch sits at ~3,050 words. Reviewers should pressure-test three places.

First: the lay-by conversation. The conversation runs ~1,400 words and is the chapter's argument-bearing scene. The risk is that the revelations land as too articulated — both characters speak in finished sentences that real people in the moment of revelation would not find. I held this with Vikram's *I do not think you should do anything about Maya. Don't change what you say to her* — the prepared sentence delivered the next morning, which is when prepared sentences arrive in marriages, not in the moment. The lay-by itself is rougher: Vikram's "Yes" repeated, the *I'm not — I'm not angry. I'm just—* truncation, the *That's not true / I told myself it was for both possibilities / Which is also not true* exchange. If reviewers find the dialogue too clean, the cut is the *Vikram did not usually ask things this way* meta-paragraph — letting the question's specialness be carried by the question itself.

Second: the closing paragraph trade. I held canon's *AIs tending soil* line by re-reading it, on the bet that the chapter's most quoted line should not be replaced silently. If reviewers find this too on-the-nose — the branch making its argument by quoting its parent — the cut is the line itself, leaving only *the marriage was a calibration too. She had not thought of it that way before* as the closing thought.

Third: the morning's *I'm closing the search* speech. Vikram delivers a four-point declaration on the terrace. The risk is that the declaration reads as too complete a closure for a chapter that has been about drift. I held it with the *I am asking you to also say some of them to me. As you find you can. Not all at once.* clause — which makes the speech a beginning, not a conclusion. If reviewers find it too tied-up, the cut is the *I want to know about the dream* exchange — letting the dream remain Meera's alone for now.
