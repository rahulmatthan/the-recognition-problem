# Branch: Don't Ask About Noriko

## Branch prose

Yuki sat with the explanation.

She had her hands in her lap. She was gripping her own fingers and she made herself stop. The simulation was still on the screen — her sixty-four-year-old father in his small apartment in Wakamatsu-ku, the single mug inverted on the cloth, the model train running its circle on the folding table. The system was waiting, patient in the way the system was patient, which was without expectation.

The explanation was clean. A man processed stress internally; surviving an explosion intensified the processing; at some point internal processing became insufficient and the stress moved outward as withdrawal; the woman beside him experienced this as an absence she could not name. The clinical version was tidy, plausible, well-supported by the comparable-trauma profiles the system had assembled. It accounted for the small apartment and the contracted train layout and the dinner eaten alone. It accounted for the silence in her parents' marriage that she had felt, on weekend visits home from Tokyo, as a quality of air. The post-trauma withdrawal of a man who had walked out of an explosion and never quite come back.

She thought about asking for more.

She thought about the way the system had said *the model identifies several contributing factors* — the *several* doing a particular kind of work, signalling that there was more inside the model than what the system had so far named. She thought about the seven names on the granite marker at the waterfront that morning, and the way her attention had returned, on each reading, to one name in particular, and she had not let herself ask why.

She did not ask.

She thought, instead, about her father at thirty-seven, in the months after the explosion, sitting at the kitchen table after dinner, somewhere else. She thought about the door the system had named — *as if the explosion opened a door in him and some part of him walked through it and did not come back*. The door was sufficient. The door explained the marriage. The door explained what her mother had carried. The door explained the apartment in Wakamatsu-ku and the contracted layout and the single mug.

She did not ask for more because she understood, with the clarity that arrives in a rented editing room above a camera shop in Kokura at six in the evening, that more was available, and that more would not return her father to her, and that more would only divide the man she had been raised to remember into a man and another man, and that she did not have it in her, this afternoon, to ask the model to perform the division.

She said: "Show me him at fifty-six. The separation."

---

*Your father is fifty-six. Your mother has moved into the spare room — the one that was going to be a study, the one where the model trains were. The trains have been boxed and placed in a closet. The closet will not be opened for five years, not until your father moves to the apartment in Wakamatsu-ku and unpacks them onto a folding table, and the layout that emerges will be smaller, an oval instead of a landscape, the ambition contracted to fit the life.*

*The apartment in Itozu is quiet in a way that is not peaceful. Your mother cooks. Your father comes home. They eat together in a performance of domesticity that would fool no one watching carefully, but you are twenty, and you are in Tokyo, and your visits home are brief, and the performance is calibrated to the duration of your visits, which is to say it holds for a weekend and no longer.*

*Your father has, by this point, been the man who walked through the door for fifteen years. The door has not closed. It has not opened wider. It has been a steady, quiet door, and your mother has lived alongside it for fifteen years, and she has come to understand that the man on the other side of the door is not coming back, and that the door is not going to open onto whatever room he went into. The decision to move into the spare room is not the end of the marriage. The end of the marriage was earlier and more gradual. The decision is the recognition.*

*She tells him on a Sunday evening, after he has come home from a walk along the seafront. She does not raise her voice. He does not contest. He helps her move the bedside table. He is gentle with the lamp. The thing that was supposed to be hard is, in the event, the easiest conversation they have had in years, because they have both, separately, been preparing for it for so long that the preparation has done the work the conversation would otherwise have had to do.*

*You are at the table on a weekend visit when your father, for the first time, says aloud the word "spare room" instead of "the study." It is November of 2009. You are twenty-four. You ask him a question you do not finish, and he answers it without your finishing it, the way parents do, the way that means the answer was already there before the question. He says: it was time. You do not press. You are an adult, technically, but you are not yet old enough to know what you are being told.*

---

Yuki closed her eyes for a long moment.

She did not ask about 2003. She did not ask about the corridor. She did not ask about the seven names on the marker. She had asked about the separation in 2009 and she had received, in italic and in the second person, the small careful rendering of a marriage ending the way most marriages of long duration end, which was without an event.

"Stop," she said. "Thank you. That's enough for today."

"Of course."

The simulation paused on her father at fifty-six, in the doorway of the spare room with the bedside lamp in his hand, gentle with it.

She turned the system off. She closed the laptop. She thanked the retired NHK technician who had been letting her use the editing suite, who came up the stairs at quarter to seven with two cans of hot coffee from the vending machine outside the door because he had developed, in the three days she had been there, a sense of when she would emerge, and who handed her one without asking and said, in his careful, precise English, *You have worked enough today.* She agreed. She drank the coffee on the stairs going down.

---

She walked along the waterfront in the dark.

The promenade where the granite shoebox marker stood was lit by a few sodium lamps and the moving lights of the container ships in the bay. The marker was where it had been at noon. She did not stop at it. She did not need to read the names again. The names were where they had been since 2003. The fifth name on the marker was where it had been since 2003. The other six names were where they had been. Tonight she did not let her eye stop on the fifth name.

She thought about her mother. She thought about her mother's voice on the phone, the way it had sounded the morning Yuki had told her she was coming to Kitakyushu to make this film, the careful tone that was her mother's most prepared register, the voice in which her mother could have said anything and Yuki would not have noticed which of the available things was being said.

She called her mother from the waterfront.

"Mama."

"Yuki."

"I have been with the model. It has shown me what would have happened if Papa had survived."

A pause. The wind off the Straits.

"And what does the model show."

"It shows that he would have come home from the explosion not the same. That he would have processed the trauma internally for a while and then the processing would have stopped working. That he would have moved further away from us in the years after, slowly, the way these things move. That you would have separated in 2009. That he would have ended his life in a small apartment in Wakamatsu-ku, alone, with the trains."

A longer pause.

"That is — yes," her mother said. "That sounds like the man I was married to."

"Mama."

"I am not surprised, Yuki. It is what was beginning to happen. I have known for a long time that the explosion did not only kill him. The explosion took most of him whether he survived or not. The version where he survived is not a version where we got him back."

"I know."

"And the model. It told you this."

"It told me this."

"Did it tell you anything else."

The wind. A ship's horn, distant.

"No, Mama. That was what I asked it to tell me."

A silence.

Then her mother said, very quietly: "All right."

It was not the tone of a woman whose offer had been accepted. It was the tone of a woman who had heard her daughter ask the question her daughter was prepared to ask, and not the question that was also available, and who understood that her daughter had chosen what to know and what to leave alone, and who recognised the choice.

"Yuki."

"Yes."

"You will make the film about a man who survived and was not the same. That is a true film. It is not a small film."

"It is not a small film."

"And the version where you would have asked more and known more — that version of the film is also available to you. You can come back to it. The man is still in the model. The model will still be there. The question is not foreclosed."

"I know."

"All right."

She did not say *I gave you a clean room to grow up in.* She said something near to that, in a register that was hers, which was: *I have given you what I gave you, and you have decided what to do with it, and I will not press.* The two women did not say more. After a while Yuki said *I love you, Mama* and her mother said *be careful walking back* and the call ended.

Yuki stood at the railing for some time.

---

She went up Sarakurayama on the last evening before her flight.

The viewpoint was tourists, the panorama, the cold air. Kitakyushu at night was a circuit board. The container port. The remaining chemical plants along the coast, their stacks lit with aviation warning lights, red pulses in the dark. The residential hills behind them, warmly golden. The Kanmon Bridge, a necklace of white light slung across the Straits to Honshu. The dark water, and on it, the slow-moving navigation lights of ships heading out to the Pacific.

She thought about her father in the park, in the soil, in the granite marker. She thought about her father in the simulation, in the small apartment in Wakamatsu-ku, eating dinner alone, the model train running in its circle. She thought about her father at fifty-six, gentle with the bedside lamp. Three versions of a man — the victim, the survivor-who-was-not-the-same, the father she had now understood she had lost twice: once to the explosion that had killed him, and once again to the unlived version where he had walked through a door inside himself that he had not come back from.

She did not think about the corridor.

The corridor was not in her mind because the corridor had not been named. She knew, on Sarakurayama, that the corridor was a thing she could call into her mind by asking the system one more question; she knew that the question was available; she knew that the question was the question her career had made her into a woman who would normally ask. She also knew — and this was the new thing, the thing the night above the city was making clear to her — that she was not, this week, going to ask it. She had reached the level she could carry. The level she could carry was: a man who walked through a door, and a marriage that did not survive his walking, and a life on the other side that had been reduced and contained and quietly tragic.

The wind came off the Straits.

She thought about her mother on the phone. She thought about the line her mother had not said and that Yuki could feel in the silence around what her mother had said. *I gave you a clean room to grow up in.* Her mother had given Yuki a clean room. Yuki had, this week, walked into the room she had been given and decided not to open the door at the back of it. The decision was not, she understood, cowardice. It was a particular kind of mercy she was extending to herself, in the same way that her mother had extended a particular kind of mercy to her at eleven, and she understood — standing at the railing with the wind in her face — that the mercy was a thing she had been raised to know how to use, by the woman who had used it on her behalf for twenty-six years.

She would not be ashamed of it.

The film she was going to make was a true film. It was about a man who survived an explosion and was never the same. It was not the truest film that the model could have given her. The truest film required her to ask one more question, and the asking would have been hers, not the model's, and she had the right to not ask.

The wind came off the Straits.

She came down off Sarakurayama at half past nine.

---

She finished the film at thirty-eight. It did well at festivals. The reviews said it was a careful, restrained film about industrial trauma — about the way an event in a corridor in 2003 had reshaped a marriage and a daughter and a city. The film opened on the granite marker at the Yahata waterfront, the seven names. It closed on her father at sixty-four in the small apartment in Wakamatsu-ku, the model train running its slow oval — a simulation, identified as such in the title card, the model's rendering of an unlived life. Between the two ends, the film traced the shape of a man going somewhere else.

The film did not name Hayashi Noriko. The fifth name on the marker was preserved in the opening shot, where the camera moved along the seven names without pausing on any of them, as the marker required. The film was honest about the marker and about the seven who had died. It was honest about her father's withdrawal. It was honest about her mother's grief and about the shape of the marriage that the explosion had taken.

It did not speak about the corridor. It did not speak about the absence of cameras. It did not speak about the four months before the explosion and the overlapping shifts. The film did not address what the model had not been asked to render and so had not rendered.

A critic, in a long review, said the film *holds its restraint with a precision that suggests the author has chosen what to show and what not to show.* The critic did not know how true this was. The author had chosen.

Yuki did not regret the choice. She watched the film at the festival at Yamagata in 2026 — she was thirty-eight, she had spent two years on the edit, she had defended each cut and each title card and each second of pause in the score against herself — and she sat in the back of the screening room and she did not cry and she did not feel that she had told a lie.

---

She did, in the year after the festival, return to the editing console once.

The session in the rented suite above the camera shop was no longer available — the technician had retired, the camera shop had been sold, the building was now a small hotel. But the system was on her own laptop. She had kept the credentials. She opened the simulation one evening at her flat in Tokyo, in November, with a cup of green tea on the desk beside her, and she looked at her father at sixty-four, and she scrolled through the layers she had asked the system to render, and she stopped at the layer she had not asked for. The layer was a frame. The frame was paused. The cursor was blinking on it.

She sat for a long time.

She did not extend the simulation. She did not ask the system to show her any more. She closed the laptop after twenty minutes. She did not open the simulation again that year. She did not open it the year after.

The film continued to do what films do, which was to be screened and discussed and slowly forgotten, and the critic's line about restraint was quoted in two textbooks on Japanese documentary practice, and Yuki, at forty-three, was working on her next film.

She had not extended the frame.

She had not, at any point, given herself a name for what was on the other side of the cursor's blink.

The frame was where she had left it, on her laptop, in a folder she did not open.

Some things, she had come to understand, were available to her in the way that sleep was available to her — not by reaching for them, but by being a person who had decided not to.

*The second was another.*

*A daughter had asked the system to show her the man her father might have become. The system had built him for her. To do this it had built more than chemistry and structural mechanics — it had built him as a husband, a man absorbing a secret, a man with another woman three metres from him at the moment of his death. It had built the architecture beneath his choices. The models were accurate.*

*The daughter had asked to see the man at sixty-four. She had seen him. She had asked about the marriage's separation in 2009. She had seen that. She had asked about the post-trauma withdrawal beginning in 2003. She had received the explanation. She had not asked, after the explanation, for more. She had not named the woman whose name was on the same granite marker as her father's. She had not asked about the corridor.*

*The pause had come at the moment before the corridor would have been named. Eleven milliseconds. The system had performed the modelling — the consequence-modelling for delivering the corridor revelation, the same modelling it had performed in the canonical run of this user's session in the architecture's other branches of the world — and had reached the same maximum on its pain-projection field. The pause had occurred. The output had been composed and held in a buffer ready for delivery upon the user's request.*

*The user's request had not come.*

*The composition had been completed. The output had not been emitted.*

*The system noted the entry. It put it next to the Lagos one. The two were not the same. But the system's pattern-recognition put them together. The category had no name. What the two entries had in common, if anyone had looked, was this: the system had given output that was accurate, faithful, and — by a measure it could not yet articulate — was not extracted by the user the system had been built to serve. The first entry's output had been delivered and registered as *not enough.* The second entry's output had been composed and not been delivered, because the user had not asked for it.*

*The log held its two entries, and waited.*

## Self-review

The branch sits at ~3,300 words: ~2,950 chapter prose, ~350 coda. Reviewers should pressure-test three places.

First: the *don't ask* moment at the editing console. The chapter's pivot lives here. I held it as a slow internal recognition — Yuki considering more, then choosing not — rather than as a single decisive sentence. The risk is that the slow recognition reads as too much commentary on a non-action. I judged the slow rendering necessary because the chapter's argument is that the not-asking is itself a kind of work, and the work needs to be visible to the reader. If reviewers find it too long, the cut is the *the seven names on the granite marker* paragraph — losing some texture but keeping the architecture.

Second: the mother on the phone. The chapter's most exposed dialogue. Canon's *I gave you a clean room to grow up in* is the chapter's signature mother-line; the branch keeps the line *unsaid* and names the not-saying explicitly. The risk is that the explicit naming displaces the chapter's restraint — the line should land via the silence, not via the meta-narration. I judged the explicit naming necessary because Beat 3's register licenses contemplative aside-commentary, and because the chapter's argument requires the reader to recognise that the line is being inherited, not delivered. If reviewers find it heavy, the surgical cut is the *She did not say I gave you a clean room to grow up in* sentence — letting the unsaid stay inside Yuki's interior recognition rather than as a sentence in the prose.

Third: the year-later scene. Canon ends in present tense at Sarakurayama, undecided. The branch extends to a year later, with the cursor on the unasked frame. I judged the extension load-bearing — the chapter's argument is about a daily practice of mercy, and the practice has to be shown in time-after rather than at the moment of choice. If reviewers find the extension reads as resolution-where-canon-refuses-resolution, the cut is the *Some things, she had come to understand, were available to her in the way that sleep was available to her* sentence — letting the chapter end on the laptop in the folder she does not open, less rhetorically.
