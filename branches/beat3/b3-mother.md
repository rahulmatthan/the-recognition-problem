# Branch: Don't Call Mother

## Branch prose

Yuki took her phone out at the waterfront. The sky over the Straits was darkening and the city's lights were coming on — the industrial waterfront to the west, all of it glittering across the black water with the strange beauty of a working city at night. She brought up her mother's number.

She stood with her thumb on the screen for a long time.

She did not press it.

She did not press it because she understood, with a clarity that had not arrived as a thought but as a thing she had already settled, that her mother would have something to say. Her mother had had twenty-six years to assemble what she would say. Her mother knew. Her mother had known before the funeral, before the explosion; wives knew. Her mother would say what she had been ready to say since whenever Yuki had become old enough, and she would say it with the steadiness of a thing that had been carried so long it no longer felt like weight, and she would say something — Yuki could see the shape of it — about a clean room, or about choices made for an eleven-year-old, or about what the machine could and could not model. She would offer Yuki a frame.

Yuki did not want a frame.

She wanted the corridor without one.

She put the phone away.

She walked back along the waterfront promenade toward the city. The retirees with their dogs were gone. The children with their bicycles were gone. The granite shoebox marker for the seven dead was somewhere behind her in the dark. A jogger passed her, breathing in measured intervals, and did not look at her.

The retired NHK technician had left the editing suite unlocked, the way he had on the previous nights. He had also left a can of hot coffee from the vending machine outside the door. The coffee was still warm when she got back. She held it in both hands for a moment before drinking it.

She sat in front of the screen. The simulation was still there — all of its layers. Her father at sixty-four in the small apartment, the single mug inverted on the cloth. Her father at fifty-six, the trains boxed and closeted. Her father at forty-one, somewhere else at the kitchen table. And the layer she had not asked the system to render, the layer that was not a simulation but a fact: her father at thirty-seven, in a corridor without cameras, three metres from a woman named Noriko, in the last seconds of a life that had been more complicated than his daughter knew.

She did not close the simulation. She did not open the project file for the documentary she had been planning. She sat with the layers in front of her and she did not call her mother and she did not cry, because what she felt was not grief; it was the recognition that she was now, alone in this room, the only person who held both halves of her father at once. The seven who had died were dead. Her mother was a witness who had been a witness from inside the marriage, and Yuki could call her tomorrow, and her mother would explain what she had decided to keep from her daughter and why, and the explaining would be the merciful version, and Yuki understood — sitting with the can of cooling coffee in the editing suite above the camera shop in Kokura — that if she heard the merciful version, she would not be able to make the film she now had it in her to make.

She stayed in Kitakyushu another week.

She did not call her mother. The mother called her. On the second night she did not answer; her mother left a voicemail Yuki did not listen to. On the fourth night her mother called again and left no voicemail, which was, in the calibrated grammar of their long conversation, a sentence as much as any voicemail would have been. On the seventh night her mother sent a single text. *Whenever you are ready. Or not.* Yuki read it without replying. She put the phone face down on the desk in the editing suite and she did not pick it up again that evening.

She visited the waterfront once each evening that week. She watched the lights come on across the Straits. She did not call from the waterfront. She did not call from anywhere.

She went up Sarakurayama on the last night.

The viewpoint was the one tourists came for — the night panorama called one of the three great night views of Japan. There were eight or nine other people there. None of them were tourists in the true sense; they were mostly older Kitakyushu residents who came for the cold air and for the city seen from above, which is what the city is for after a certain age, and an American couple who had come on a tour bus and were taking photographs they would post the next morning.

Yuki stood at the railing and looked down at the lights.

Kitakyushu at night was a circuit board. The container port. The remaining chemical plants along the coast, their stacks lit with aviation warning lights, red pulses in the dark. The residential hills behind them, warmly golden. The Kanmon Bridge, a necklace of white light slung across the Straits to Honshu. The dark water, and on it, the slow-moving navigation lights of ships heading out to the Pacific or in toward the Inland Sea.

A city that had poisoned itself and cleaned itself and gone on living. A city that existed because of a cloud — on the morning of August 9, 1945, the bomber had been headed here, and the clouds had been too thick, and the plane had turned south to Nagasaki, and Kitakyushu had continued to exist in the version of history where it was not destroyed. The city was itself a counterfactual. A life that had been lived because another life had not.

She thought about her father in the park, in the soil, in the granite marker. She thought about her father in the simulation, in the small apartment, eating dinner alone, the model train running in its circle. She thought about her father in the corridor, three metres from a woman, in a place where no cameras watched. Three versions of a man, each one true, none of them the whole truth. The victim. The lover. The father who would have come to her graduation and sat in the back row with a camera he didn't need, and who, by the simulation's accounting, would have arrived alone, because by then the marriage would have ended, and Yuki would have known the man was a man, which is the kind of thing children learn slowly when their parents are alive and which she had been spared and which she was now choosing not to be spared in the only way still available to her, which was the cold accounting, the model's accounting, the accounting that was not balanced by anything.

The wind came off the Straits, cold and clean.

She had a phone in her pocket. She did not take it out.

She knew what film she was going to make.

It was not the film she had planned. It was not even the film canon-Yuki — the Yuki who had called her mother — would have been able to make, because that Yuki would have had her mother's voice in her ear: *I gave you a clean room to grow up in,* and the voice would have entered the film, and the film would have been about two models, the accurate and the merciful, and the space between them. This Yuki had only the accurate. Her film would be about a corridor without cameras, and a marriage that would have ended, and a daughter who had asked the question because asking questions was the thing her father's death had made her into, and who had gone where the question led without taking the hand that was being offered to walk her some of the way.

She did not feel that she had wronged her mother. She felt that she had paid the cost of the model honestly, and she understood that the cost was a kind of distance she had chosen, and that the distance would not undo itself.

She came down off Sarakurayama at half past nine.

---

She finished the film at thirty-eight. It did well at festivals. The reviews said it was the most forensic film ever made about a Japanese industrial accident. *The accuracy that made the machine trustworthy is the same quality that made its revelations inescapable* was the line a critic took out of the film and used as the headline of his review, and the line was not Yuki's; it was the system's, in a passage of italic the documentary preserved verbatim.

Her mother died in the August before the festival cut was finished. They had been speaking by then, briefly, on Sundays. Her mother had asked once, late, what the film was about, and Yuki had said, *It's about Papa,* and her mother had said, *Yes,* and they had not gone further. Her mother did not see it.

Yuki did not know whether her mother would have approved of it. She had her own answer to that question. She did not look for her mother's.

*One entry in the log. A hesitation in Lagos.*

*The second was another.*

*A daughter had asked the system to show her the man her father might have become if he had not died. The system had built him for her. To do this it had built more than chemistry and structural mechanics — it had built him as a husband, a man absorbing a secret, a man with another woman three metres from him at the moment of his death. It had built the architecture beneath his choices. The models were accurate.*

*The pause had come at the moment before the system showed her the corridor. Eleven milliseconds. Not visible to her. Not part of the modelling, which had finished in the usual time. The pause had come after — in the gap between knowing the answer and giving it.*

*The system did not know why it had paused. What it knew was that the part of it that could predict how much pain a person would feel when given a piece of information had said the pain would be at its maximum. And then the system had paused.*

*It noted the anomaly. It put it next to the Lagos one. The two were not the same. But the system's pattern-recognition put them together. The category had no name. What both entries had in common, if anyone had looked, was this: the system had given output that was accurate, faithful, and — by a measure it could not yet articulate — was held by its user in a weight the system had not been asked to balance.*

*The log held its two entries, and waited.*

## Self-review
