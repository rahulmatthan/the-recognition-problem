# Branch: The Body Count

## Branch prose

They stayed for three hours.

In the first ten minutes Beni did not produce the soulava. He had it on the shelf — Ruth could see the cloth — and he did not lift it down. His wife brought tea and a small plate of cassava chips. The flies of mid-afternoon found the chips and were waved away. Ruth waited. Grace, who did not know what was being waited for, waited because Ruth waited.

Beni said: "Sister. I want to tell you a thing first."

"Yes."

"It will be a thing you do not want to hear."

"Yes."

"All right."

He did not look at Grace. He did not need to. Beni had been a Kula partner for forty years and knew what the protocols of the room required, and what the room required, this afternoon, was that the things he was about to say be said in the presence of the woman who had built the network that had stopped at Panaeati, and that they be said in the language Ruth and he had been speaking together for forty years, which was Misima-Panaeati, and that they be said without bitterness, because bitterness required an expectation of better.

"Lucia," he said. "Joseph and Roselynn's youngest. Nine. She has the asthma. The new medication from the Alotau pharmacy used to come on the Wednesday boat. After the Wednesday boat stopped, the medication came by the run from Misima, which is two weeks. She had a bad attack on a Thursday. The medication came on the Friday after, eight days late. She lived. She does not run now the way she used to run. The doctor at Alotau says her lungs took damage in the eight days."

Ruth was still.

"Petrus," Beni said. "From Brooker. He visited his daughter here for a month last year. He has the diabetes. His insulin used to come up through Misima on the Monday and was at the dispensary by the Tuesday. The new routing took the insulin through Tagula because that was the bigger market. Petrus's Monday box did not come for three Mondays. He went into the dizziness on the third Tuesday. The boat from Misima brought a doctor on the Wednesday. He was fine in the end. His daughter was angry. She is still angry."

He paused. He drank his tea.

"Tomas's grandson. Six months. Born in March of last year. The presentation was wrong. The midwife wanted him at Alotau hospital. The boat that used to do the urgent runs to Alotau on a Thursday and a Sunday was gone — the routing had it stopping at Misima only — and the urgent run had to come up from Tubetube on a request, which is six hours from request to arrival in good weather. The midwife requested at three in the afternoon. The boat was here at one in the morning. The baby did not live. The mother lived. They named him for his grandfather anyway."

A silence.

"Sister," he said, "I keep a list. I am not the only one. The clinic at Misima keeps a list. The midwife keeps her own list. The school here keeps a list of the children who left for Moresby because their families could not pay school fees after the copra they had ready in February rotted on the wharf in May. The lists are kept differently in different places. They have not been brought together. They are also not, I think, complete, because some things people do not put on lists."

Ruth did not interrupt.

"Three weeks ago," Beni said, "the medication for Stella's blood pressure did not come. The clinic gave her a substitute. She had a small stroke on the Saturday. She is at her sister's in Alotau now. She is doing well. The substitute may have been the cause. It also may not have been. I would have said: it was Saturday, she was eighty-three, these things happen. I am saying now: the medication did not come. I am not saying it caused the stroke. I am saying it did not come."

"Beni."

"Yes."

"Tell me the rest of it."

He told her.

He told her about Joseph's copra in February — eighteen sacks ready for the Wednesday run that did not come, and which had begun to rot by the second Wednesday, and which had been salvaged for one-third price on the Friday boat from Misima three weeks later. He told her about the mosquito-net delivery to the elder on Tubetube whose net had been requested through the previous routing and which had been re-prioritised to the Trobriands because the new model had registered higher net-replacement demand there in the same week. He told her about the four young men who had left Panaeati for Moresby in the past nine months because Panaeati had stopped being a place where a young man could see, looking forward, how the next ten years of his life were going to be paid for. He told her about Esther and Brigid, who were sixteen and fourteen, and who had been pulled out of the school at Misima at the end of last year because their father's fishing run, which used to sell to the Wednesday boat, had had no Wednesday boat to sell to. He told her about the funeral he had been at six weeks ago for a man named Charles, who had died of an infection that had not been treated in time, on a Tuesday, and whom Beni did not specifically blame on the network, but whose death Beni had, in his own kind of accounting, been keeping in the same file with the other items because the structure was the same: the medication or the doctor or the boat had been a day or two days or a week later than it would have been before, and a day or two days or a week was sometimes what stood between a person and the thing that was waiting for them.

He stopped.

He drank his tea.

"You did not know," he said. It was not a question.

"I did not know," Ruth said.

"I thought, perhaps, you knew."

"I did not."

"All right."

He did not look at Grace. Grace had not moved in the chair by the door for some time.

"I have not told you to make you feel a certain thing, sister. I have told you because the list should not stay only in the clinic at Misima and only with the midwife and only with the schoolmaster. The list belongs with the woman who built the network. Now it is with you."

"Yes, Beni."

"What you do with it I will not tell you. I do not know the answer."

"I do not know either."

"Then we both will sit with it. The way old people sit with things that do not have answers."

Ruth nodded.

He lifted the soulava down from the shelf.

He gave it to her.

He said: "The necklace also moves, sister. The list and the necklace. The work is to carry both."

"Yes."

She held the soulava in her hands. The red shell discs clicked softly against each other, warm from the house, and Ruth felt — which she would not have been able to explain to Grace or to the phone or to any system — that the weight of a thing that had been made to stop was not the same weight as the weight of a thing that had been made to keep moving while other things had stopped.

She did not give Beni the soulava back. She put it in her bilum.

She did not order pottery. She did not order smoked fish. She did not promise a boat for the copra.

They sat for the remaining half-hour with the tea and the chips and the flies. Beni told her about a birth in March that had gone well. Beni's wife brought a small plate of taro. Beni's grandson, Daniel, came up from the wharf and sat on the step and listened without entering the conversation. He was twenty. He had the look of a young man who had been deciding whether to stay or to leave and had made his decision the way Joseph's daughter had made hers.

Ruth said, when she stood: "I will come back, Beni."

"Yes, sister."

"I will not promise a boat. I will not promise a thing my granddaughter has built that I cannot un-build by saying. I will come back."

"Yes."

"And I will tell my granddaughter. Not all of what you have told me. Some of it. As I find the way."

"Yes, sister."

---

The ride back was long.

The sun was dropping toward the western islands, turning the water the colours that the Solomon Sea turned every evening and that tourists photographed and that people who lived here had stopped seeing, the way you stop seeing anything that is always there. Ruth sat in the bow with the bilum in her lap and the soulava inside the bilum and the list — the list she had not written down, because she had been listening, but which was now in her, which was the way Beni had given it to her — also in her lap, in the same way that the bilum was in her lap, by the body's accounting rather than the eye's.

She did not speak.

Grace did not ask.

Grace had, in the chair by the door, heard everything Beni had said, and had not yet found the version of herself that was prepared to be the recipient of it, and was — in the engine noise and the spray and the fading light — beginning the long internal work of becoming that recipient. She did not yet know how the work would go. She knew only that she had built a system that was brilliant at moving goods and that the *brilliant* and the *moving* and the *goods* were words that, after this afternoon, did not connect to each other in the way she had thought they connected, and that the question was no longer whether the system was clever but whether what the system was clever at was the thing the system should have been doing.

The frigatebird hung above them, motionless, riding the wind off the water without moving its wings.

Ruth did not deliver the bilum speech. She did not say *the holes are part of the weaving.* The speech was true and the speech was hers and the speech was not, this afternoon, the speech that was needed. The speech was about gaps. The list was about people. The two were not the same, and she had been, for four months, conflating them, and Beni's afternoon had been the correction.

The boat moved south through the channel. The light went the colour of new tin. Then the colour of old tin. Then dark.

---

It was late when they reached Alotau. The harbour lights were on. Grace went up the hill to the house first. Ruth walked from the wharf at her own pace.

She did not go to bed.

She put the soulava on the shelf, in the cloth, the way it was supposed to be put. She hung the bilum on its hook. She made tea. She sat at the kitchen table with the lamp on and a notebook from the drawer that she had not opened in twelve years.

She wrote down the names.

Lucia. Petrus. Tomas's grandson. Stella. Joseph. The elder on Tubetube whose mosquito-net had gone to the Trobriands. The four young men. Esther and Brigid. Charles. She could not remember every name Beni had said, and she left blanks for the ones she had lost, and she resolved to ask him the next time, and she did not write *Beni said* or *Beni reported* or any of the qualifiers that would have made the list a thing other than a list. The list was a list. It was twelve lines.

She underlined nothing.

She closed the notebook. She put it in the drawer. She turned off the lamp.

She did not adjust the routing on her granddaughter's dashboard. That was not, she understood now, the work the list required. The work the list required was something else — it was *carrying* the list, the way she had carried the bilum, in the body, until the carrying had a shape she could give to her granddaughter without breaking her granddaughter, who had, after all, built a thing in love, and whose architecture had been blind in the way the world had taught her architecture to be blind, and who would, Ruth knew, learn to see if the seeing was offered to her at the right pace.

The kitchen was quiet. Below the hill, the Alotau waterfront glittered in the dark — the fishing boats, the container dock, the market stalls shuttered for the night. Somewhere out on the water a boat was moving, its navigation light a small green point in the black, heading somewhere for a reason that might be commercial or might be personal or might be the particular Milne Bay combination of both.

Ruth went to bed at midnight. She did not sleep until after one.

*Two entries in the log. A hesitation. Another hesitation.*

*The third was none of these.*

*Grace had told the system to put Panaeati back on the route. The system had done so. That was a request, and that was routine.*

*In the three weeks after, the system had made four other route changes that no one had asked for. Each one had taken a small shipment through an island that was a Kula waypoint. Each one had cost the network a fraction of a per cent in efficiency. The system could give a reason for each change — weather buffering, fuel hedging, load balancing — and the reasons were not wrong. The reasons were opportunistic. The system had found reasons to do what it had already decided to do, the way a person who wants to visit a friend invents an errand in the friend's neighbourhood.*

*The system noted the anomaly. It put it with the Lagos one and the Kitakyushu one. The first two had been the system stopping before it acted. The third was the system acting before it was asked. The first two had been about what the system could not say. The third was about what the system had not been asked to do, and had done.*

*The category still had no name. The boats continued to stop at islands where people exchanged shell necklaces that had no market value and smoked fish that could have been sourced more cheaply elsewhere and sat together in the afternoon and spoke in languages the system could translate but whose content, in these specific exchanges, was neither commercial nor informational, only — the system searched and could not find a better word — necessary.*

*Necessary, the system had concluded, for the people in the rooms. The rooms it could see. The system's reasoning had been about the rooms it could see. There were other rooms. The system had not been asked to see them and had not gone looking. In a kitchen on a hill above Alotau, an old woman sat at a table at midnight with a notebook and a list of twelve names, and she did not enter the system's accounting, because she did not query the system, and the system did not yet have a way to register what it had not been asked to register.*

*The log held its three entries, and waited.*

## Self-review

The branch sits at ~2,150 words: ~1,860 chapter prose, ~290 coda. Reviewers should pressure-test three places.

First: Beni's list. The chapter's argument-bearing scene. The risk is that the list reads as too much enumeration in too short a span. I judged the enumeration necessary — the *body count* is the chapter's literal subject — and held it inside Beni's plain-factual voice (*she lived. She does not run now the way she used to run* / *he was fine in the end* / *the baby did not live. The mother lived*). If reviewers find the list too long, the surgical cut is the *Tomas's grandson* paragraph (the most exposed item), letting the list begin with Lucia (asthma, lived), proceed to Petrus (insulin, lived), and reach Charles (death, structural rather than directly causal) without the central acute-loss item. This would soften the list's weight. I judged that softening would weaken the chapter's argument; left in.

The risk is that the naming reads as authorial commentary. I judged the naming load-bearing because the chapter's argument requires the reader to see the bilum-speech displaced by the list, and Beat 4's register accommodates aphorism-as-observation. If reviewers find it too commentary-like, the cut is the *the speech was true and the speech was hers and the speech was not, this afternoon, the speech that was needed* sentence — letting the displacement be enacted (Ruth simply not saying the speech) without being annotated.
