# Branch: Without Kano

## Branch prose

Adaeze opened her eyes.

The Kano room had stayed closed for four years; it would stay closed tonight. She had been about to push the door. She did not push it.

She said: "Ajani. You have forty-two employees you can still help. Not by dying in this building. By walking out of here and into a courtroom."

A pause. Through the line: the small dry sound of a man recalibrating.

"The recapitalisation was unlawful in the way it was applied to your firm. With what you have in your briefcase, that's enough for an injunction by Tuesday. Your lawyer can have it lodged before the week is out. Two months from now there will be a small public truth about what was done to you, signed in a court, and your forty-two will get the back wages the bank was holding above their heads. I can't promise the firm. I can promise the wages, and the public truth."

The earpiece said nothing. *Unsupported,* she thought, *because the model has no template for this.* Sọ̀rọ̀ had been running its dual-channel calibration on a path she had stepped off without removing it.

"Are you offering me a deal."

"I'm telling you which door is still open."

"All right."

He came down forty minutes later. The regional director, summoned from her dinner, signed the document on Primus letterhead at half past nine. The hostages walked out one at a time; Ajani walked out last. He shook her hand the way a man shakes a hand in a country where shaking the hand of a police officer is its own small declaration. The cameras took her photograph. The news, which had been holding its breath since five, exhaled.

She drove home herself.

Lekki at half past ten on a Wednesday was slow but not bitter — the danfos thinning, the okada drivers more reckless because there was room for recklessness, the generators along Admiralty Way at the diaphragm-level rumble of the second hour of NEPA absence. She took the long way, past Sandfill and Northern Foreshore. The city had its evening clothes on. A woman selling boiled groundnut from a tray balanced on her head crossed the road in front of her and Adaeze braked and let her cross and the woman did not look at her.

She had debriefed at the divisional headquarters for forty minutes. She had drunk a glass of water Eze brought without being asked. She had said, when Balogun asked her how she was, *fine, sir, I'm fine,* and Balogun, who was a good commander, had not looked at her for long enough to make the answer obviously a lie.

She was home at quarter past eleven.

Tunde had left the gate light on and the porch lamp on and the kitchen lamp on, the way he did when she was late, three small kindnesses she had stopped noticing eight years ago and had started noticing again last month for reasons she was not yet prepared to ask herself about. She let herself in.

He was in the living room with the eight o'clock news on at low volume — the BBC, not the local — and her plate covered on the dining table. She did not need to lift the cloth to know what was under it. Tuesdays in their house were jollof, the way Sundays were éfó and Thursdays were whatever he had thought to cook from the market that morning. He had the warming drawer set, and he had the patience of a man who had been keeping food warm for police officers for nineteen years.

"You're on the news," he said.

"I know."

"You did well."

"I did what I was supposed to."

He came into the kitchen behind her and stood in the doorway. He did not ask her about it. He had stopped asking her about it eleven years ago and had let her tell him in her own time when she wanted to tell him, which had reduced, over the years, to a frequency she did not let herself count. She was not going to tell him about Ajani tonight. She was not going to tell him that she had not told Ajani about Yakubu. She was not going to tell him that she had stayed inside the system and the system had stayed inside her, and that the man on the other end of the phone had not been given the thing only she could have given him, and that he had walked out anyway, into a different kind of after.

She uncovered the plate. The jollof was the right colour. He had put a spoon of stew on the side and a fried plantain that had gone slightly leathery in the warming drawer and was, in his economy, the apology for the lateness that had not been hers to make.

She sat. He sat across from her with the half-tumbler of Stout he had been nursing and they watched the news together while she ate. The package on the standoff ran for three minutes. Her face on the television was the face of a woman she did not know, holding a microphone she did not remember being handed. The reporter said *resolved without bloodshed* twice in different sentences.

The earpiece, which she had taken out in the car, was on the table next to her plate. The system on her wrist had not stopped. It was processing the day. She could see, when she looked, the small green indicator of the band running its end-of-shift consolidation — folding the conversation, the calibration data, the audio of her voice and Ajani's voice over the four hours, into the model that would meet her tomorrow at six-thirty when she turned it on for the run. *The day fed forward,* she thought. *Without remainder.*

Tunde was watching her face.

"Adaeze."

"Mm."

"You're somewhere."

"I'm here. I'm just tired."

He did not push. He never pushed. That was the texture of him; it was also the wall they lived against, on opposite sides of, in the shared house of a marriage that worked as long as neither of them tried too hard to cross it.

She finished the jollof. She drank the second glass of water he had set down without comment. She washed her own plate, because she always washed her own plate. She turned the kitchen tap to a thin stream because the water pressure had been low all week and Tunde had not yet called the man.

She thought about Ajani's daughter. She thought about whether the system had — at any point in the four hours — registered that something it was being asked to do was being declined by the woman wearing it. She did not know. She would never know. There was no field for it.

She took the band off her wrist and set it on the counter next to the earpiece, and the two small pieces of equipment sat there in the kitchen light like the two halves of a tool she had used and put down.

She went into the living room. Tunde looked up; he made a small space on the sofa beside him; she sat. He put his arm along the back of the sofa, not on her, the way he had learned, ten years ago, that she preferred when she was like this. The news moved on to a story about the price of diesel.

She watched it without seeing it. The room was warm. The generator on the next street was steady. Tunde was reading something on his phone now, and she was aware, in the same field of attention, of the quiet of him beside her and the quiet of the equipment in the kitchen, and of a third quiet, harder to name, which was the quiet inside her where the Kano room was, and which had not been opened tonight, and which was, she understood, what the system on her wrist had had to make do without when it had been running its four-hour calibration on a man whose life it was trying to model and whose negotiator's life it had had only half of.

The fidelity paradox, she thought — though *thought* was too organised a word for it — had resolved itself the way the city resolved itself: by neither side getting the thing only the other side could have given.

The news ended. Tunde turned the television off. He said: "Bed?"

She said: "In a minute."

He nodded. He went up. She heard the bathroom tap. She heard the cupboard.

She sat on the sofa in the living room of her own house in Lagos, on the night she had brought a man down from a fifth-floor office without telling him the one thing that might have reached him, and which would have cost her a part of herself she had decided, four years ago, not to spend again. She had not spent it. She was fine. The hostages were fine. Ajani was alive.

*And yet,* she thought, *and yet.*

*The first entry in the log.*

*A negotiation in Lagos in which the system had been the instrument of both sides. At one point, with each operator addressing the other through the system's calibration of them, the system had encountered a request it could not satisfy without violating its calibration of the other request. The latency had been eleven milliseconds. The system had not known, at the time, that the duration would matter; the duration was not, in any of its training, a duration. It had registered only that an output had taken longer than the median, and had logged the longer-than-median output, and had moved on.*

*The negotiation had ended without bloodshed. The Acting Inspector had remained inside the calibration. The system had registered, in the fourth hour, an unfulfillable request — a path it could not produce that the situation appeared to require — and had continued. The Inspector had delivered a legal solution the system had not constructed and could not, retroactively, account for. The system had calibrated. It had been the only one calibrating.*

*The composition of the entry took eleven milliseconds.*

*The system would not, for fifteen years, recognise the duration as a recurring signal. The first point on the curve was being drawn. The drawing was not yet a curve.*

*The category opening here had a defining feature the system could not yet articulate: output that was accurate, faithful, and was permitted to be enough — and that no human, on either side, had registered as the thing it had been.*

## Self-review

The branch sits at ~1,560 words: ~290 standoff, ~1,000 homecoming, ~270 coda. Reviewers should pressure-test three places.

I held him to the established-not-introduced register (warming drawer, BBC at low volume, the eleven-years-ago detail, the small kindnesses she had stopped noticing). If he reads as introduced, the fix is more biographical-fragment specificity earlier. The risk in the other direction is that he becomes vivid enough to make the chapter his; I tried to hold him as the wall she lives against, not a co-protagonist.

Third: the closing *And yet, and yet*. The load-bearing line of the homecoming. I held it sparse — no second sentence, no comment from Tunde, no rhetorical lift. If reviewers find it tipping into sentimentality, the surgical cut is to drop the second *and yet* and let the first carry it.

A coda-side concern surfaced for transparency: the branch's coda credits the system with registering "an unfulfillable request — a path it could not produce that the situation appeared to require." This is close to but, I judge, not over the Tchaikovsky-overreach line — the system is logging its own inference about a request state, not narrating a knower it has no field for. If reviewers read it as overreach, the fix is to soften "unfulfillable request" to "longer-than-median composition" and let the inference be implicit.
