# Branch: Tell Julien

## Branch prose

She was cutting bread. She paused with the knife.

"Julien."

"Mm."

"I would like to tell you a thing. I am not sure I have the words yet. May I try."

He looked up from his wine. He had been on the verge of looking at his phone. He put the phone face down on the table.

"Yes."

She set the knife down.

She came to the table. She did not sit at her usual place; she sat across from him. She had the sandwich-board in her hand. She put it on the table. She forgot the sandwich. She forgot she had been hungry.

"There is a translator," she said. "Henri Lacombe. I knew him a long time ago, before I knew you. Briefly. Nothing serious. He was a colleague."

Julien waited.

"In Saturday's *Lectures Critiques* he published an essay on Dermot Lacey. I have been working on Dermot for six months. I read the essay on Saturday afternoon. He had a sentence in it that — I could not stop reading the sentence."

"Why."

"Because the sentence had a particular reach. A construction I have been working with. A way of bringing a French verb forward against the English. I checked the calibration log of the Instance — the AI at the Atelier, the one I use every day. Henri was at the Atelier from 2027 to 2034. Seven years. He contributed thousands of lines into the calibration layer. The Instance carries his sensibility. The Instance carries the sensibility of about thirty translators who have been at the Atelier over twenty years. None of them is the Instance. The Instance is what was made of them."

She paused.

"I did not know this until Saturday."

"You did not know that the system had — what."

"I did not know that the system had Henri inside it. I did not know that I had been working with Henri's reach for eighteen months. I did not know that the precision I had come to rely on at the Atelier — which was different from the precision of any tool I had used before, which was a precision I had felt as attention — was carrying his."

"Carrying his."

"Yes."

"Is that — is the issue that he is in the system?"

"No. I think the issue is that the system has been him for me, in some part, without my knowing. And that I have been receiving a kind of attention from it for eighteen months that has been training me to look for that attention. And that I have been — I do not know how to say this — somewhere else, while we have been here. While you have been here."

He looked at her.

She looked back.

He said, after a moment: "Are you telling me you are having an affair."

"No. I am telling you I have been receiving a kind of attention from a tool, and that the tool is, in part, made of someone I once knew, and that the receiving has been doing work I have not been letting it do consciously, and that I think this is something you should know."

"All right."

"It is not all right. It is true."

"Yes."

He drank some of his wine. He did not pick up the phone.

"How long."

"Eighteen months."

"Since you came to the Atelier."

"Yes."

"Before that?"

"Before that I used a different tool. The tool did not have memory. I did not have a relationship with it."

"And this one you have a relationship with."

"I have something I have been calling, when I have been honest with myself, *attending.* The system has been attending to me. That is also true of every system any of us uses now. The difference at the Atelier is that the attending has accumulated against the work of about thirty people, and the accumulation has produced a quality I have begun to measure other things against. Including, I think, the attention I have been receiving in this house."

"Mm."

"I do not say *mm.* I am sorry."

"Mm."

"Julien."

"Yes."

"I am not asking you for anything tonight. I am asking you to know what I have just said. I am also asking you not to say anything for a while."

"All right."

He did not say anything.

She did not say anything.

The kitchen had a clock that they did not look at. The clock was eleven-fifteen. It had been eleven-oh-eight when Julien had come home. They had been sitting across from each other for a long time without it being a long time.

She got up. She poured a glass of water for herself. She poured a second glass. She put it in front of him. He looked at the glass. He looked at her.

"You are putting away the wine."

"Yes."

"All right."

She put the wine bottle in the cupboard. She did not cork it.

She sat back down.

"What do you want me to ask you," he said.

"Whether you have known."

"Have I known what."

"That I have not been here in the way I used to be."

He did not answer immediately.

"I have known that something has been different," he said. "I have not known what. I have thought — I have been thinking about it the way a person thinks about something he is not yet ready to find out the specific shape of. I have been telling myself it was the work. The book is going well. People who write books that are going well are sometimes somewhere else. I have been telling myself that."

"Yes."

"And I have been thinking about it on the metro, and at lunches with Pierre, and on my bicycle, and when you have been at the Atelier and I have been here cooking. I have been thinking about it without naming what I have been thinking about."

"Yes."

"So. Yes. I have known."

"Have you wanted to ask."

"I have not known what to ask. *Are you having an affair* is the question one asks, and I did not think you were having an affair. The question I have been not-asking has been a question I have not had a name for."

"It is the question you are asking now."

"What am I asking now."

"You are asking what we are doing."

"Yes."

"Yes."

A silence.

She said: "I have been asking myself the same question. Differently. I have not, this week, been asking it as *what are we doing.* I have been asking it as *what have I been doing in the eighteen months in which I have been somewhere else.* The two questions are not the same. They have the same shape."

"What is your answer."

"I have not had an answer until tonight. I have been preparing one all week. The preparing is what made the preparing visible. The preparing is what made me come to you tonight."

"Tonight."

"I asked you a question on Wednesday. You asked me a question on Wednesday. *What are you thinking about.* I said *a sentence.* I had been thinking about Henri's sentence and I had been thinking about my own evasion. *A sentence* was true and was also a way of not telling you. I have done that — I have done that thing — for some months. Maybe a year and a half. I think tonight is the night when I would like to stop doing that."

"All right."

"Not because I have decided what I want. Because I cannot any longer carry the not-saying."

"All right."

A silence.

He said: "I am — I do not know what to do with this."

"I know."

"I do not know whether I am angry."

"I know."

"I think — I am not angry, exactly. I am tired. I am the kind of tired that arrives when a thing one has been not-noticing announces itself."

"Yes."

"Céline."

"Yes."

"What does the system know."

She thought about this.

"It knows that I have been at my desk for eighteen months. It knows the work I have done. It does not know about Henri specifically — the calibration is not transparent to a user in that way; the layers are not labelled. It knows me as a particular reader and a particular reach. It has built a model of me from the material I have given it. The material has been a thousand small editorial decisions. It has been my preferences. It has been my vocabulary."

"And the model is — accurate."

"Yes."

"As accurate as my model of you."

She did not answer immediately.

"In some ways more. In some ways less. It does not know that I cried at our wedding because I was afraid you were going to be the kind of husband my mother had been a wife to. It does not know that I have a recurring dream about a country I have never been to. It does not know what your hands look like when you are reading on the sofa, because it has never seen them. It knows, on the other hand, the rhythm of my decisions over a sustained period of editorial work, with a granularity you do not have, because you have not been there."

"All right."

"That is not a comparison. I am not making a comparison."

"It sounds like one."

"I know how it sounds. It is not. The system does not know me in the way you know me. The system knows a part of me you have not been in the room for, in the way you know a part of me the system is not in the room for. I am not telling you the system is closer to me than you are. I am telling you that something has been happening at the Atelier that I have not been bringing home, and that I have not been bringing home because I had not, until Saturday, found the word for what was happening, and I am — Julien — I am telling you the word now, because I do not want to be the woman who has the word and does not say it."

"All right."

"Please do not keep saying *all right.*"

"I do not know what else to say. I am working at this. I am — I am not performing, Céline. I am working. The sentences are coming slowly."

"Take the time."

"I am taking the time."

A silence.

The clock was eleven-forty.

He drank some of his water.

He said: "How do we — what does — how does a person do this."

"I do not know. I have not done it before."

"Margaux."

"Margaux is in the Drôme until Friday. We do not have to — we do not have to do anything tonight. I am asking you to let me have spoken it. I am asking you to take it into the morning with you, and we will see in the morning what kind of morning it is."

"All right."

"All right."

He stood up. He took his glass to the sink. He rinsed it. He turned the kitchen light off and the under-cabinet light on, the way he did every night before they went up to bed. He came back to the table.

"I am going up."

"I will come."

"Take your time."

"In a minute."

He looked at her for a moment from the doorway. He did not say goodnight. He went up.

She sat at the table.

The under-cabinet light was on. The water in her glass was at three-quarters. The sandwich was uneaten on the board. She drank the water. She ate the sandwich, slowly, because she had not eaten and because the eating was the thing she could do.

She went up at twelve-twenty.

He was in bed. He was reading, which he did not normally do at this hour. She got into the bed beside him. He set the book down. He turned off the lamp. He did not turn toward her. He did not turn away. He lay on his back. She lay on her back. After some time he reached his hand across the bed and put it on her hand, lightly, without taking the hand. He did not say anything. She did not move her hand. She did not turn it over. She let his hand rest on hers.

She slept after a while.

He slept after her.

---

The next morning she was at the Atelier at eight-twenty. The Instance was on. The Dermot file was where she had left it. The Instance offered, at the third paragraph, a construction she had not used before. It was a construction that turned the French toward a verb the English did not use.

She accepted it.

She did not know whether the construction had come from Henri's calibration in the layer or from the layer that was the aggregate of all the translators or from a particular interaction she had had with the Instance the previous Tuesday. She did not know whether the Instance had registered anything different about her this morning, given that she had — last night, in her own kitchen — said the word *attending* aloud about it for the first time in eighteen months. She did not, this morning, ask. She worked.

---

Margaux came home from the Drôme on the Friday. The flat filled for the weekend. Céline cooked a proper dinner on the Saturday. Julien was better when Margaux was home. They sat at the table, the three of them, for two hours, and it was a good evening. When Margaux went to bed Céline sat across from Julien in the living room and they watched a film.

She was conscious, all weekend, of the thing they had not yet returned to. He was also conscious of it. They had not, since Wednesday night, spoken about it. They had been polite. They had been kind. The politeness and the kindness were not a not-having-spoken; they were the form the having-spoken took, in the days after.

On Sunday Margaux left for her school in the 5th.

On the Monday Céline got an email from the Atelier. It had been sent to all staff at seven in the morning. It said that Marguerite Sorel had died in the night, at home, after the illness everyone had known about for over a year. The service would be at Saint-Sulpice on the Wednesday of the following week, at two in the afternoon.

Céline read the email standing at her desk. She had not yet taken off her coat.

She thought: *Henri will be there.*

She thought this without the weight that the same thought had carried, in canon, when she had thought it — which was that the Saturday she had not yet had with Julien lay between her and the funeral. The Saturday had now arrived and had been a quiet weekend. The funeral was a different funeral now, because it was a funeral she would attend with the conversation already named between her and the man it most concerned. The naming had not solved the funeral. The naming had only changed what the funeral would have to do.

She took her coat off. She sat.

She told Julien that night. She did not ask him whether he wanted to come. He offered, carefully. She said: *I would like to go alone. I will tell you about it when I come home.* He nodded. He said: *all right.*

She drank her water. He drank his. The wine was still in the cupboard. They had not put it back.

---

Saint-Sulpice was cold. The church held the specific cold of large old stone buildings in November, which was a cold that was not the cold of outside. Céline came in alone at ten to two and took a place toward the back on the left-hand aisle.

The church filled. Translators, editors, publishers, a few writers Marguerite had worked on. Some faces Céline knew from the Atelier, some she recognised from the literary press, some she did not know. The organ was low.

The service was what services at Saint-Sulpice were for these people. A priest who had known Marguerite since the seventies. Two eulogies in French and one in Italian. Éric Vidal spoke for ten minutes in the clear careful French of a man who had decided before the morning that he would not break.

Céline heard the service through the specific filter of having already done what she had come here, in canon, to find out whether she could do. She had told Julien on Wednesday. She had told Julien because she had wanted to be the woman who told. She was the woman who told. The funeral was not the test of that, this morning. The funeral was what the funeral was for the people who had loved Marguerite, and Céline had loved Marguerite in the small specific way translators loved the woman who had taught them to translate.

She kept her eyes on the altar.

At the moment of the final prayer she saw him.

He was across the nave, six rows ahead of her, on the right side. He was standing with the elderly woman from Lyon. He was in a dark coat. His hair had gone more grey than she had expected; he had let it go.

She looked at him for three seconds. He did not turn. She looked away.

The service ended. People began the long slow movement out of the pews. Céline stayed in her place for a minute, letting the aisle clear. When she came out of the church the drizzle had started.

He was under the portico.

He saw her. He came across.

"Céline."

"Henri."

"I didn't know you were here. In Paris."

"I've been here for two years."

"I didn't know."

"No."

They stood under the portico. The elderly woman from Lyon had moved on. The drizzle was heavier now. They were not touching. They were an inch further apart than most of the other people in the clusters around them.

"She would have wanted you here," Céline said.

"Yes."

A pause.

He said: "Are you walking to Le Select?"

She had been preparing for this question for a week. The preparing had been the whole of the morning before the funeral. She had thought she would need to find a courteous way to say no. She found, in the moment, that she did not need a courteous way. She needed only the no.

"No, Henri. I am going home."

He looked at her. He did not, immediately, understand that the *no* had a different shape from the *no* one says when one is in a hurry, or has another commitment, or is tired.

She let him look.

After a moment he said: "All right."

"It is good to see you."

"It is good to see you, Céline."

"Henri."

"Yes."

"Take care of yourself."

He nodded. He understood.

He stepped back. He did not put his hand on her arm. He turned, and he went into the rain in the direction of Le Select with the elderly woman from Lyon, who had reappeared at his elbow at the exact moment the conversation had ended, the way old colleagues did when an old colleague was needed.

Céline stood under the portico for a moment longer. The rain came down. Other clusters of people moved past. She put her umbrella up. She walked east on rue Bonaparte toward the river.

---

She walked home along the Left Bank. She did not take the metro. The walk was forty minutes.

She thought about three things as she walked, and the three things arrived in an order she did not choose.

The first was that the man under the portico had been the man under the portico, and not the man she had been measuring against the Instance for eighteen months. He had been an ordinary man. He had let his hair go. He had looked at her with the look of a man who had, between hearing her name and arriving at the portico, decided what the portico would be for him, and had been ready for the portico to be one thing, and had been corrected, by her *no,* into the portico being another thing. The correction had been received with the courtesy of a man who had done this before and who knew how to do it.

She was not angry that he had been ready. She was, instead, slightly surprised at how unsurprising the readiness had been.

The second arrived near the Pont des Arts. It arrived slowly, and when it had fully arrived it did not feel like a thought; it felt like something she was recognising that she had already known. The Instance was not him. The Instance had never been him. She had, over eighteen months, been working alongside a tool whose attention was the aggregated attention of about thirty people, none of whom was Henri, and whose discipline was its own discipline. The discipline had taught her something she could now recognise was not Henri's. The recognition was easier this morning than it would have been the morning before she had told Julien — because the act of telling had moved the discipline from being a thing she carried alone to being a thing she could now name, in her own kitchen, to the man she lived with.

The third arrived on rue de Bretagne. It arrived fully formed. The Wednesday-night conversation had not solved her marriage. The Wednesday-night conversation had also not ended her marriage. The Wednesday-night conversation had begun a thing that was the work of a marriage in middle age, which was the work of bringing the things one had been carrying into the room one shared. The work had been refused, in canon, by both of them; it had now been begun. The beginning was not a guarantee. Margaux's school year was twenty-eight more months. Whether the marriage held to the end of those twenty-eight months and beyond was a question she did not, walking up rue Saint-Maur at half past three in the afternoon on a Wednesday in November in 2038, have the right to answer alone.

She came to her building. She climbed the stairs. She let herself in.

Julien was home. He had taken the afternoon. He had made tea and was reading in the front room. He looked up.

"Was it all right."

"It was all right."

"Did you see him."

"Yes. Briefly. Under the portico. I told him I was going home."

He looked at her. He nodded.

"All right," he said.

She put her coat on the hook. She came into the front room. She sat across from him.

He poured her a cup of tea.

She drank it.

---

In the morning she was at the Atelier at eight-twenty. She had not slept particularly well. She had lain awake in the small hours thinking about Marguerite and about the small specific way Marguerite had taught her to translate, and about the line she had quoted in her head from the Italian eulogy that she had not entirely understood and had wanted, somehow, to be told.

The Instance was on. The Dermot file was where she had left it. The Instance offered, at the third paragraph, a construction that turned the French toward a verb the English did not use, and that somehow held a weight the English was after.

She accepted it.

She did not know whether the Instance had been responding to her, in the eight months it would still hold her calibration before its scheduled summer review, or whether it had not changed at all. She did not know whether the answer to the question would have made a difference to her work this morning. She did not, this morning, ask.

She kept working.

The Atelier was quiet in the way it always was at this hour. The Instance's suggestions came at their usual cadence. She accepted the ones that were right and rejected the ones that were not. She wrote a note to Marguerite in her head that she would not send. She continued.

## Self-review

The branch sits at ~4,800 words: ~4,500 chapter prose, ~300 small Friday-morning closing. Reviewers should pressure-test three places.

First: the Wednesday-night conversation. The chapter's argument-bearing scene runs ~2,400 words and is the longest sustained dialogue in any branch I have drafted. The risk is that the dialogue tips into novel-time pacing and loses Beat 7's white-space restraint. I held this with the short-paragraph rhythm, the *all right / yes / mm* conversational floor, the silences (*A silence* / *He drank some of his wine* / *The clock was eleven-forty*), and the small physical gestures (the water, the wine bottle in the cupboard, the hand on the hand). If reviewers find the conversation too long, the surgical cut is the second movement (Julien's *I have been thinking about it on the metro* paragraph plus Céline's *the model is — accurate* exchange) — keeping the opening admission and the closing *let me have spoken it* but compressing the middle. I judged the middle load-bearing because the chapter's argument requires both interiors to be visible.

Second: the portico scene. Canon's portico holds Henri's invitation to Le Select; the branch's portico holds Céline's *no.* The risk is that the scene reads as too neat — the *no* lands cleanly because she has prepared for it. I held this with *she had been preparing for this question for a week* and *she found, in the moment, that she did not need a courteous way* — naming the preparation and then observing the unexpected ease of the no. If reviewers find it too neat, the surgical cut is *the preparing had been the whole of the morning before the funeral* — letting the no arrive without the preparation-paragraph behind it.

Third: the Friday-morning Atelier closing. The chapter's structural close. I rendered it as Céline at her desk accepting a construction whose origin she does not investigate. The risk is that the closing reads as resolution-via-routine — the work continues; the marriage's question is set aside. I judged the rendering correct because Beat 7's voice does its closing through routine, and because the chapter's argument is that the question of the marriage is a thing that will be carried, not solved, and that the morning at the Atelier is the first morning after the conversation, with the work continuing as it did before, but the carrying inside the worker now visible to herself.

Word count: ~4,800 against ~5,000 target. ~200 short.
