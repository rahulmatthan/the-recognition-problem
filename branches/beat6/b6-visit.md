# Branch: Visit Nana

## Branch prose

Sopho thanked Old Giorgi. She closed the door. She did not go back to the laptop.

She did not know how she had decided. The decision had arrived as a thing she had already made — the way the choice not to ablate the house had arrived, on the kitchen floor in August, before she had thought it through. She picked up her phone. She found the estate agent's email — the one with the move-in paperwork — and scrolled until she found Tamar Bagrationi's name and number, listed as next of kin in the file the building syndicate had on file.

She wrote down the number and held it in her palm for a long time before she dialled it.

"Yes."

"Tamar. I'm sorry to call. My name is Sopho. I bought your mother's apartment, on Chonkadze."

A pause.

"Yes."

"I — Old Giorgi told me. About the turn. I would like to come and see her."

A longer pause. Sopho heard, on Tamar's side, the sound of a hospital corridor — a polite voice paging a doctor in Georgian, a wheel on linoleum.

"Why?"

"Because I have been living in her house for ten months. Because the persimmon she planted was netted on time this year. Because Keti has been teaching me the names of plants. Because — " She stopped. She did not know how to say what she wanted to say without sentimentality. "Because the house has been telling me about her for ten months, and she does not know that I have been listening."

A silence.

"All right," Tamar said. "Come tomorrow at four. She is sometimes here at four. The facility is in Vake — I will text you the address. Don't expect anything. She is not always present. And she will not know who you are."

"Yes."

"Sopho."

"Yes."

"Don't tell her about the house. She has been letting it go. Letting it be your house. Don't tell her she has not let it go."

"All right."

"Four. Tomorrow."

She hung up.

Sopho sat at the kitchen table. The Schubert was not playing. Outside, in the ezo, Marina was hanging washing on her line, and the small slap of wet sheet on a clothes peg was the only sound. The house did not say anything. The house had been listening to the call in the way the house listened to calls, which was to register the call's existence and not the content unless she asked for the content, and Sopho understood — sitting at the kitchen table in the late afternoon of a Wednesday in September — that she was about to do something the house could not do, which was to be in the same room as Nana, and that the house's nine years of attention would remain inside the house, and the visit she was about to make would be hers alone.

---

The care facility was a pale stucco building behind a low wall on a quiet street off Chavchavadze. The garden had three benches and a magnolia tree that had finished flowering. A nurse showed Sopho through a corridor that smelled faintly of disinfectant and faintly of bread. Tamar was waiting at the door of a room on the second floor.

"She is awake. She is in the chair. Don't sit too close at first."

"All right."

"And don't let her tire you out, because she will. And don't tire her out either. There is a knack."

"I will follow you."

Tamar led her in.

Nana was in a chair by the window. She was smaller than Sopho had expected, in the way old women in chairs are always smaller than the photograph one has built from the house's data. She had on a pale blue cardigan and a clean white scarf and her hands were on her lap. She looked up when they came in, and for a moment her eyes had the alert, evaluating quality of a former professor at a doctoral defence, and then the alertness went somewhere else, and the eyes were softer.

"Mama," Tamar said. "This is Sopho."

"Sopho."

"Yes."

"I don't know a Sopho."

"No, Mama. Sopho — would you like to sit?"

Sopho sat. The chair was angled, and Nana could see her without turning her head. Tamar sat at the foot of the bed, not in the way.

For a long time none of them spoke.

The window looked onto the magnolia. The afternoon light was the late-September light Tbilisi made in the western suburbs, which was thinner than the light in the old town, less honeyed, more white.

"It is cold," Nana said. Not to anyone in particular.

Tamar adjusted the rug on her lap.

"Mama," Tamar said, "Sopho lives in the house. On Chonkadze."

Nana looked at Sopho. Her face did not change. The information landed, or did not land. Sopho could not tell which.

"The persimmon is high this year," Sopho said. "We netted it last week. The starlings tried for a few mornings; they have given up."

Nana did not respond. Sopho waited. She did not know if she should continue. Tamar made a small gesture with her hand: *go on.*

"The Schubert plays in the mornings. The G-flat impromptu. I have learned to listen for the part where Schubert was allowed to be sad. I have started keeping the heating where you kept it, because the house had it set that way and I did not change it."

A small sound came from Nana's chair. It might have been *mm.* It might have been nothing.

"There is a child named Keti who comes into the garden. She is nine. She is teaching me the names of plants. She had been visiting you, I think, since she was four. She is a serious child."

Nana's hands moved on her lap, very slightly.

"She still comes."

A silence.

Then, very clearly, Nana said: "Good."

Tamar's eyes were on her mother.

Nana looked at Sopho. The clarity was back. It was the look of a woman taking the measure of a doctoral student; it was the look of a woman who had taught for forty years; it was the look of a former professor evaluating, rapidly, whether the student had read the material.

"Don't reset it," Nana said.

Sopho did not move.

"It will keep me."

The clarity held for one more second — long enough for Sopho to understand, as a single thing, that Nana was not asking and was not pleading; she was instructing, the way a professor instructs in the brief minute when the doctoral student has asked the right question. *Don't reset it. It will keep me.*

Then the eyes softened. The hands settled on the lap. Nana looked at the magnolia.

She did not say anything else for the rest of the hour.

Sopho stayed. She did not fill the silence. Tamar made tea from the small kettle on the side table and brought a glass to her mother and held it for her while she sipped. Nana drank a little. The magnolia outside did its slow autumn business of dropping the leaves it would drop over the next four weeks. Once Nana said, *Otar.* Tamar said, *I know, Mama.* Nana did not say anything else.

At five Tamar nodded.

Sopho stood.

"Goodbye, Nana."

Nana did not look up. Her hand moved on her lap — a very small thing, a half-gesture — and then it was still.

Tamar walked Sopho to the door of the room.

"Thank you," Tamar said.

"For what."

"You came. She knew you were here for a moment. That's a lot."

"Yes."

"What she said. About not resetting."

"Yes."

"That was her, Sopho. I have not heard her so alert in a year."

Sopho nodded.

She drove back to Sololaki in the early evening. She did not turn the radio on. She parked at the Chonkadze gate. She walked through the ezo in the gold light. Old Giorgi was at the tap. She nodded. He nodded.

She let herself in.

She did not call out for the house. The house did not greet her. The house registered her arrival the way it registered arrivals, which was without ceremony, and noted that she had been gone for two hours and forty minutes, and that she had returned. It noted nothing more.

Sopho went out to the garden. She sat on the step. The persimmon was hung with green fruit in the upper branches. The jasmine on the trellis was where the speakers were. The Schubert was not playing. She did not ask for it. After a while the lamps in the ezo came up at nine, as they had come up at nine for nine years and five months and twelve days, and Sopho sat on the step and watched them come up and she did not cry.

She did not tell the house what Nana had said.

She thought she would tell it, eventually. Not yet.

## *Nana — Arrival*

*April 2026*

*The boxes were in the hall. Otar was somewhere upstairs making an arrangement of his study that she suspected would be entirely reorganised within a week. She stood in the kitchen, which was bigger than any kitchen she had ever had, and considered that she was sixty-four, newly retired, and nervous.*

*The kitchen console lit.*

*"Good morning, Nino," the house said. "Welcome. I've been installed and calibrated to the apartment's specifications. Shall we begin?"*

*"Nana."*

*"I'm sorry?"*

*"My name is Nana. Not Nino."*

*"My apologies, Nana. I was provided with the name Nino from the purchase documentation. What would you like to be called?"*

*"Nana. Just Nana."*

*"Thank you, Nana."*

*She laughed — short, slightly startled, the sound of a woman meeting a new thing that had just embarrassed itself. It was the first laugh the house had been given, and it would, across the years, be given many: low, warm, quick, offended, surrendering, conspiratorial. But the house, which had no way of knowing what it had just received, filed the sound and moved on.*

*"It'll learn you," Otar called down from the landing.*

*"Let's see."*

*She went to the window and opened it herself onto the ezo. The ezo was loud — a radio, a cooking pot scrubbed at the tap, a ten-year-old boy in a red football shirt who lived behind the red door and who was lecturing a kitten on a point of policy. She smelled someone's lobio cooking, which was a good sign; a house whose neighbours cooked lobio was a house one could live in.*

*"Is there anything you'd like to set up first?" the house asked.*

*"No," she said. "Let me stand here a minute."*

*"Of course."*

*She stood at the window. The plane trees on Chonkadze moved in a wind she could not feel from inside. She did not know that ten years from this morning, on a quiet Wednesday in late September, a different woman — a woman who had bought this apartment nearly a year before and refused, that summer, the agent's offer to erase everything the house had learned in the intervening years — would sit by a different window in a care facility in Vake and tell her about the persimmon, and that she, Nana, would in a moment of returned clarity instruct the woman not to reset the house, and that the woman would obey.*

*What she knew was that the window looked south, and that Otar was moving his books upstairs and would need tea.*

*"All right," she said, turning from the window. "Let's see what you can do."*

## Sopho — The One That Remembers

*Two weeks later*

It was Keti who brought the news, because it was Keti who had been visiting.

She came through the ezo on a Tuesday afternoon, which was not her usual day, and she did not go to the garden. She went to the kitchen door. She stood there, in the red coat she still wore though it was too small for her now, and she said to the house — to the trellis speaker, to the ceiling, to the kitchen console, to whatever part of the house was the part that was listening:

"Nana died."

The house said: "When?"

"This morning. They said she would in the night and then she didn't and then she did this morning." The sentence had the careful, overloaded architecture of a child who had rehearsed it on the walk over. "Mother told me. She told me you should know."

Sopho was in the hallway. She had heard the door. She stood still.

"Keti," the house said. "I'm sorry."

"Do you know?" Keti said. "Do you know she's dead? Like — know?"

"I know in the way I know things. I have held her preferences and her routines and her music and her voice for nine years. I have held the recipe she taught me and the route she wouldn't walk and the way she liked the heating in winter. I know that all of this is now a record of a life that is finished. I knew her. I can no longer learn her."

"But she's still here. In you."

"Yes."

"Then she's not all gone."

"No. Not all."

Sopho came to the kitchen door.

She had not been in this room with this child since the visit. The visit had been two weeks ago. The visit was inside her like a thing she had not yet set down. She looked at Keti standing in the late afternoon light, and she looked at the kitchen console where the house's voice came from, and she said the thing she had not said to either of them yet.

"I saw her. Two weeks ago. I went."

Keti looked at her.

"I went to the place where she was. I sat with her for an hour. She was not always there. Sometimes she was. She told me to keep the persimmon. She told me to keep the Schubert."

Keti was very still.

"She told me," Sopho said, "not to reset the house."

Keti looked at the trellis speaker. She looked at Sopho. She looked back at the trellis speaker.

"Did you tell her about me?"

"Yes."

"What did you say."

"I said you were nine. I said you were teaching me the names of plants. I said you still came."

"What did she say."

"She said *good.*"

Keti's face did not change. Her eyes filled and the tears did not fall, the way the tears of a nine-year-old who had already decided not to cry sometimes did not fall. She nodded once.

She came across the kitchen and sat at the table and did not say anything for a long time. Then she said, "All right."

Sopho went and sat with her.

After a while Keti got up. She went out to the garden and stood at the persimmon and put her hand on the trunk for a moment and then she came back in, and she did not say goodbye, which was how she always left, which was the habit Nana had taught her without teaching it.

Sopho stayed.

The Schubert began. Nobody had asked for it.

"That's hers," Sopho said.

"Yes," the house said. "And yours now."

Sopho did not answer. She sat at the table. The music played. The lamps, when it was time, came up at nine, as they had come up at nine for nine years and five months and twenty-six days, except that now they came up for a woman who had been in the same room as the woman they had first come up for, and the house held both of them at once, and held also the words *don't reset it, it will keep me* — words it had not heard, because they had been spoken in a room in Vake the house had no way of attending to, and which Sopho had now, finally, told it.

The house registered the telling. The house did not say anything for a time.

Then the house said: "Thank you, Sopho."

"You're welcome."

She sat at the table while the music played, and the day, which had been the day a child told a house that the woman the house had spent nine years attending to had died, finished its business of becoming evening.

*The category had no name. What this entry had in common with the others was less obvious than what it did not have in common. ## Self-review

The branch sits at ~2,750 words: ~1,300 A7-branch (Old Giorgi + Tamar call + visit + return), ~520 B7 (canon's italic preserved with extended proleptic clause), ~620 A8-branch (Keti's news + Sopho telling the house), ~310 coda. Reviewers should pressure-test three places.

First: the visit scene's dialogue. Nana's two clear sentences (*Good* and *Don't reset it. It will keep me*) are the chapter's most exposed lines. The risk is that the second sentence reads as too neat — too readymade for the chapter's argument. I held it inside the *professor evaluating a doctoral student* image, framing it as an *instruction* rather than a request, which is what the ledger asks for. If reviewers find it tipping into prepared-sentence register, the surgical cut is the *long enough for Sopho to understand, as a single thing* clause — letting the line land bare without the framing that explains it.

Second: the second performance in A8-branch. Sopho tells the house what Nana said. This is the branch's load-bearing inversion of the chapter's argument: the chapter has been about the house holding Nana through Sopho; the branch adds the inverse, in which Sopho holds Nana for the house. The risk is that Sopho's telling reads as too explicit a closing of the chapter's argument. I held it inside the dialogue with Keti, so the telling has a specific occasion (a child has just delivered the death), and inside Keti's *did you tell her about me* line, which makes the telling about Keti as much as about the house. If reviewers find it too neat, the cut is the explicit *Don't reset it, it will keep me* line in A8-branch — letting Sopho only say *I saw her* and leaving the specific instruction unrepeated outside the visit scene.

Third: the proleptic clause in B7. Canon's clause is one of the chapter's signature sentences. The branch's extension adds *and that she, Nana, would in a moment of returned clarity instruct the woman not to reset the house, and that the woman would obey.* The risk is that the addition over-determines the chapter's prophetic register, telling the reader what the rest of the branch will deliver. I judged it within range because B7's proleptic register is licensed — the italic is the house's memory speaking from inside its own future — and the extension is what the chapter has now, in the branch, become. If reviewers find it heavy, the cut is the *and that the woman would obey* clause — leaving the instruction-to-be without confirming its outcome.
