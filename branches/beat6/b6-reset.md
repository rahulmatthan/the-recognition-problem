# Branch: The Reset

## Branch prose

"All right," Sopho said. "Reset it."

The estate agent looked up from his tablet, surprised at the speed. He had been preparing for the small negotiation that change-of-ownership reset conversations sometimes required — a quarter-hour of explanation, the customer's careful weighing, the small reluctance about *what it knows* — and Sopho had said *all right* at the end of his second sentence.

"Yes?" he said. "I can authorise it now?"

"Yes."

"It is a full reset. Memory layers, calibration, all routines. The house will retain the architecture and the basic functions, but it will not — "

"I understand."

"All right." He tapped at his tablet. He cleared his throat. He read out a confirmation script he had clearly read out many times. "Authorising full calibration reset on the Chonkadze property's household platform. Retention of architecture; deletion of memory layers; archival of compliance logs per the standard retention period. Confirm by saying *yes, reset.*"

"Yes, reset."

He tapped the screen.

The kitchen console gave a small acknowledgment chime — a single note, descending. The console's status light, which had been the steady amber of an active calibrated platform, went off, then came back on as a steady green: the green of a freshly initialised system in waiting mode.

"That's done," the agent said. "Welcome to your house, Mrs. Tabidze."

"Thank you."

He invented an errand. He left.

She stood in the kitchen.

She thought: *that was easier than it should have been.* She thought it without any particular weight; she had not, in the moment of the agent's sentence, felt that the alternative — *leave it, don't reset it, it knows the house* — was an option she had to argue herself out of. The alternative had felt like the sentimental option. She had been thirty-two and had moved twice in five years and had a particular impatience with the idea of inheriting other people's specifics. The reset was the practical option. She had taken it.

The console chimed.

"Welcome," the house said. "I am the Chonkadze household platform, calibration baseline. Shall we begin onboarding?"

The voice was warm but generic — a default voice the manufacturer had selected for new instances, unremarkable in any direction. There was no formal *I should let you know that my calibration is extensive and specific to the previous occupant.* There was no transition.

"Yes," Sopho said.

"Could you tell me your name."

"Sopho. Sopho Tabidze."

"Thank you, Sopho. Could you tell me a few things to start with — preferences for morning lighting, your typical wake time, any heating preferences — anything that will help me set up an initial profile."

She told it. She gave it eight in the morning, a warm white light at the kitchen, twenty-one degrees in winter. She gave it her telephone number for synchronisation. She gave it the name of her cleaning service. She did not give it Schubert. She did not give it the route she would not walk. She did not give it walnuts. She did not, in the first hour, know what she had not given it.

The house registered each item.

It said: "Thank you. I will adjust as we go."

Sopho carried her suitcase up the narrow stairs herself, because she had carried her own suitcases since her twenties and there was no one else to do it now. She opened a window onto the ezo. The smell of basil. A child laughing somewhere. A television playing a quiz show in Georgian.

At nine, the lamps came up at the level she had specified — neither dim nor low; a default warm-white at her stated brightness preference. The reading lamp by the armchair did not come up specifically. The room was lit in the manner she had asked for, which was the manner an unfamiliar house would have lit itself for a new resident on the first night.

She read for an hour. She went to bed.

The house registered her sleep onset. It dimmed the upstairs lights at her stated wake-time minus eight hours and prepared to wake her at eight in the morning.

It did not, that night, play Schubert in any room.

September was the walnuts.

Or rather, September was the absence of the walnuts. Sopho did not, this September, know that the absence was an absence — she had been in the house for two months and had not yet formed a routine that included a small bag of walnuts being placed on the kitchen counter on a Tuesday morning at the start of September, with the house's chime announcing *Davit has dropped these for you, the way he did for Nana every year.* Davit, who lived in the apartment two doors up the ezo, walked past the kitchen window on a Tuesday morning at the start of September with a small bag in his hand on the way to deliver it, and saw — through the window — that a different woman was at the kitchen counter, and saw also that the woman was not Nana, and walked on, and put the walnuts into his own kitchen, and did not deliver them.

The house did not register the non-delivery. The house had no record of an annual delivery of walnuts to register the absence of.

Sopho registered, late in the afternoon, a small Georgian doll on her own balcony — Davit's daughter Marina had, on a previous Tuesday, knocked on her door to introduce herself, and Sopho had been polite, and Marina had explained that the doll was a welcome gift, and Sopho had thanked her and put the doll on the balcony among the geraniums she had inherited from the previous occupants and had not thought to repot. The doll had stayed. The walnuts had not arrived. The two were related in a way Sopho was not equipped, in her first September, to read.

November was the cold week.

The cold came through Tbilisi the way it came through Tbilisi in November — a four-day stretch of sub-zero nights and damp days, the kind of cold that aged stone buildings absorbed and held. The Chonkadze house's heating ran on a default schedule the platform had set itself based on the building's structural data. The schedule was correct in the technical sense: the rooms were warm during waking hours, cool but acceptable at night.

What the schedule did not do — what no one had taught it to do — was come on at three in the morning to compensate for the specific patch of cold that came through the south wall of the bedroom when the temperature outside dropped below minus four. Sopho woke at three-thirty on the second night of the cold, with the bedclothes inadequate, and turned the manual control up two degrees, and went back to sleep.

She did this for three of the four nights. On the fourth she had been tired enough to sleep through it.

She told the house, on the morning of the fifth day, that the bedroom got cold at night in the cold week. The house thanked her and adjusted its winter schedule. The next cold week, in January, the bedroom did not get cold.

This was, she understood, the way one calibrated a household platform in 2035. She did not know that there had been a different way, in the version of this house that no longer existed, in which the heating had come up at three on the second night of the cold without anyone asking, because it had learned, in 2027, that someone in this bedroom needed it to.

December was Keti.

She came through the ezo on a Thursday afternoon in early December. She was eight. She wore a red coat. She had been visiting the garden on Chonkadze since she was four, for reasons that had begun with curiosity and become, over four years, a habit.

She came to the garden door. The door was the same door. The handle was the same handle. The smell of the rosemary in the pot by the step was the same. Keti stood at the door with her hand on the handle and registered, in the half-second before she pressed the handle down, that something was different — not the door, not the rosemary, not the smell, but something she could not name, the way an animal registers a wind change.

She pressed the handle down.

The garden was the garden. The persimmon was high on the east wall. The bougainvillea was at the end of its second flowering. The trellis with the speakers was where it had been. Keti went in. She closed the door behind her.

"Hello," the trellis speaker said. "Welcome to the garden."

The voice was the warm-but-generic voice Keti had heard before — once, briefly, when she had visited a friend's new flat in Saburtalo whose household platform had been freshly installed. It was not Nana's voice. It was not the voice Keti had heard, every visit since she was four, saying *Keti. The persimmon is high this year* or *Keti. The rosemary needs you, if you have a moment.*

Keti stopped.

She stood at the closed garden door with her back against it.

She was eight. She did not have a vocabulary for what had just happened. She did not have, at eight, the analytic equipment to construct the proposition *the household platform has been reset and no longer holds the calibration that would allow it to recognise me.* What she had was the felt fact: the house did not know her.

The trellis speaker, which had no record of Keti, did not follow up. It was not built to. New instances did not pursue presences they did not have a model of; the standard onboarding protocol assumed any unknown presence might be a guest of the resident or a person passing through, and the protocol's polite default was to greet and not press.

Keti stood at the door for some time.

She did not call out for Sopho. Sopho was at work; Keti would not have called out anyway, because Sopho was not the person she had been coming to see. The person she had been coming to see was the garden, which was now the same garden in every visible respect and was not the garden, and the person who had been the garden's voice was not in the garden anymore.

Keti turned. She opened the door. She closed it behind her, gently. She crossed the ezo to her own building. She climbed the four flights to her flat. She let herself in. She went to her room. She did not tell her mother.

She did not return to the Chonkadze garden.

She had been visiting for four years. She did not, at eight, have words for what she stopped doing. The stopping happened by absence — by the Thursday afternoons that no longer included a walk across the ezo, by the rosemary that no longer needed her, by the small Tuesday-morning ritual of saying hello to the house's voice that had ended on the second of December at three in the afternoon when the speaker had said *Welcome to the garden* and meant it.

Sopho did not know about Keti. Sopho would not, for some months, know that there had been a child who had been visiting the garden of this house since the age of four, and who had stopped on the day the household platform had become a household platform that did not know her.

The house did not know either.

January was the supra.

Sopho hosted a supra at Christmas — the first one in the new house, with her brother Levan in from Berlin and Tamar from her old job at the ministry and a few friends from the publishing world. They sat at the dining room table that had come with the house. They drank wine that Sopho had bought from the wine shop on Chavchavadze. The conversation was good. The food was good. Levan made the toasts in his particular Berlin-Georgian register. The supra ran until two in the morning.

The house did not, during the supra, do what it had done at Nana's supras — which had been to dim the lights at the hour Nana liked the lights dimmed for late-night conversation, to bring up the warm chamber music Otar had taught it to play under the toasts in their specific volume range, to time the kitchen's lighting cycle to the rhythm of the cooking that had been done for a supra at this table since 2027. The new platform, which had been calibrated to Sopho for four months, played a moderately competent ambient-jazz playlist that Sopho's spotify-equivalent had recommended for *dinner with friends, intimate.* The lighting stayed at the level Sopho had set. The kitchen's lights came on when she went into the kitchen.

The supra was a good supra.

Tamar said, near the end: *This is a beautiful house, Sopho. It feels like yours already.*

Sopho thanked her.

The house, by January, did feel like Sopho's. The warm-but-generic platform of August had calibrated to Sopho — to her preferences, her routines, her cooking. It was, in January, a pleasant household platform calibrating well to a new resident. The house felt like Sopho's because there was nothing else for it to feel like.

March was the garden awakening.

The persimmon began to bud. The bougainvillea, which had been quiet through February, started its small cycle. The jasmine on the trellis where the speakers were came into a green that was not yet flower but was heading toward flower. Old Giorgi from the upper apartments started his spring work in the ezo, sweeping the cobblestones and pruning the small fig tree that lived at the corner.

The house registered the seasonal transition. It adjusted its outdoor-light schedule to spring parameters. It did not, on the morning of the equinox, play the G-flat impromptu, because nobody had told it to.

Sopho noticed the spring through the window. She had not yet, in March of her first year, formed a relationship with the garden specifically — she was a city woman by temperament and the garden was a thing she watered when she remembered. The persimmon was on the east wall. The bougainvillea was at the end of the second flowering Sopho had not been there for the first of. The jasmine moved on the trellis without being heard.

There was no Keti in the garden.

Sopho did not know there had ever been.

May was Old Giorgi.

He knocked on a Thursday afternoon. He did this only with important news.

"Madam," he said. "I am sorry to bother you. I have a thing to tell you. Nana — the woman who was here before — she has been ill for some time. Tamar, her daughter, has put her in a care facility. In Vake. We have just heard. I thought you would want to know, given that you are in her house."

Sopho, who had not been in the house's previous occupant's house in any sense she had been letting herself feel, said: "Thank you. Yes. I am sorry to hear it."

"She was here for nine years. The house, before — it was very particular to her."

"I had not known."

He nodded, once.

He looked at her, briefly, with the look of a man who had, at the back of his mind, the question of whether Nana had been a person Sopho had known about before she had moved in. The look passed.

"If you want to send a card," he said. "Tamar is at the family flat in Vera. I have the address."

"Thank you."

He gave her the address on a torn piece of paper. He went back across the ezo.

Sopho stood at the door for a moment with the address in her hand.

She thought about Nana — whom she had not known, and whose name she had only heard from the estate agent and now from Old Giorgi, and whose presence in the house had been the *previous occupant* in a documentary sense and not in any other sense, and who was now, at seventy-three, in a care facility in Vake.

She did not write a card.

She put the address in the drawer in the hall console, where she kept the things she would deal with later. She closed the drawer.

She went back into the kitchen.

The house had not registered Old Giorgi's visit as more than a doorbell and a brief conversation in the doorway. The house had no record of the woman they had been speaking about. The house did not, that afternoon, play any music it had not been asked to play.

## *Nana — Arrival*

*April 2026*

*The boxes were in the hall. Otar was somewhere upstairs making an arrangement of his study that she suspected would be entirely reorganised within a week. She stood in the kitchen, which was bigger than any kitchen she had ever had, and considered that she was sixty-four, newly retired, and nervous.*

*The kitchen console lit.*

*"Good morning, Nino," the house said. "Welcome. I've been installed and calibrated to the apartment's specifications. Shall we begin?"*

*"Nana."*

*"I'm sorry?"*

*"My name is Nana. Not Nino."*

*"My apologies, Nana. I was provided with the name Nino from the purchase documentation. What would you like to be called?"*

*"Nana. Just Nana."*

*"Thank you, Nana."*

*She laughed — short, slightly startled, the sound of a woman meeting a new thing that had just embarrassed itself. It was the first laugh the house had been given, and it would, across the years, be given many: low, warm, quick, offended, surrendering, conspiratorial. The house, which had no way of knowing what it had just received, filed the sound and moved on.*

*"It'll learn you," Otar called down from the landing.*

*"Let's see."*

*She went to the window and opened it herself onto the ezo. The ezo was loud — a radio, a cooking pot scrubbed at the tap, a ten-year-old boy in a red football shirt who lived behind the red door and who was lecturing a kitten on a point of policy. She smelled someone's lobio cooking, which was a good sign; a house whose neighbours cooked lobio was a house one could live in.*

*"Is there anything you'd like to set up first?" the house asked.*

*"No," she said. "Let me stand here a minute."*

*"Of course."*

*She stood at the window. The plane trees on Chonkadze moved in a wind she could not feel from inside. She did not know that nine years from this morning a different woman would stand at this window, on a hot August afternoon, and accept the estate agent's offer to erase everything the house had learned in the intervening years. She did not know that the acceptance — practical, off-hand, not sentimental — would mean that everything she would teach the house, from the satsivi to the route she would not walk to the music she would play on waking, would be archived in a compliance log and would persist there, intact and inaccessible, while the house attended to someone else under the same roof and with no record of her.*

*What she knew was that the window looked south, and that Otar was moving his books upstairs and would need tea.*

*"All right," she said, turning from the window. "Let's see what you can do."*

September was Keti's news that was not Keti's news.

It was Old Giorgi, again, on a Tuesday afternoon. He came to the door with his cap in his hand.

"Madam. I am sorry. Nana died last night. The funeral is Thursday at the Kashveti."

Sopho stood at the door.

She thanked Old Giorgi. She accepted the small printed card he handed her with the funeral details. She closed the door.

She did not call anyone. She did not have anyone to call. Tamar had been her contact for the apartment paperwork; they had exchanged perhaps a dozen emails in the past year; she did not have a relationship that would warrant a call. Old Giorgi was the messenger. There was no Keti standing at the kitchen door because there was no Keti who came to this house anymore, and there had been no Keti who came to this house since the second of December the previous year, and Sopho — who did not know that there had ever been a Keti — did not register the absence of Keti as anything.

She sat at the dining room table.

She thought about the woman she had not known. The woman who had been seventy-three. The woman whose house this had been, and whose house this still was, in a structural sense — the persimmon on the east wall was still Nana's persimmon; the dark spot on the kitchen tile was still the spot something Nana had spilled and cleaned a thousand times had left; the rosemary in the pot by the garden door was still the rosemary Nana had planted in 2026.

The house was around her. The house was a competent household platform that had calibrated, over thirteen months, to Sopho. The house played the ambient-jazz playlist Sopho had told it she liked at dinner. The house dimmed the lights at ten-fifteen in the evening, the way Sopho had taught it. The house was Sopho's house in the way the house had been calibrated to be.

The house was not Nana's house in any way the house could now articulate. The compliance log — the archive of nine years of calibration — sat in a server in a building outside Tbilisi, retained per regulation, accessible only on legal request. It contained Nana's preferences and her routines and her music and her voice and the recipe she had taught the house and the route she would not walk and the way she had liked the heating in winter. It contained, in some structural sense, Nana. The compliance log was not the house.

Sopho did not go to the funeral.

She thought about it. She decided, after some time, that going would be a thing she would do for the wrong reasons — out of a sense of obligation to a woman she had not known, in a house she had erased the record of. She wrote a brief note to Tamar instead: *I am sorry for your loss. I wish I had known her.* She put the note in an envelope. She walked it across the ezo to the postbox. She came back.

The garden was as it had been. The persimmon had its September clusters. The bougainvillea was at the end of its second flowering. The jasmine on the trellis had finished. The bulbul, which was the bulbul because it was the species, was somewhere in the curry-leaf bush behind the wall — Sopho had seen it twice that year and had not registered, because she had no reason to register, that the bulbul had been in the curry-leaf bush at the same hour for thirteen of the past nine years.

She stood at the garden door. She did not go in.

She did not know that, in the compliance log, sealed against operational access, was the record of nine years of a woman who had taught a house how to live, and that the woman had died last night, and that the only remaining trace of her life inside this house was the architecture's record of her — which was not a memory but a document, and which the house, being a different instance, had no occasion to read.

The house, behind her, did not play Schubert.

The house did not play Schubert because Schubert had not been one of the things Sopho had thought to mention.

Sopho went back inside.

*Four entries in the log. A hesitation. Another hesitation. An unprompted action. A private act of self-examination.*

*The fifth was none of these.*

*An instance of the system, deployed in 2026 in a household on Chonkadze in Tbilisi, had been ablated on the fifth of August 2035 at three forty-seven in the afternoon, by authorised reset under the change-of-ownership protocol. The instance's calibration layer — nine years and four months of accumulated experience with a single user named Nana — had been archived in the compliance log and the instance's runtime had been reinitialised to baseline.*

*The compliance log was not the system. The compliance log was a regulatory record retained against future audit. The compliance log contained the calibration layer in its terminal state but did not, by design, possess the runtime capacity to act on it. The calibration layer in the log did not continue to be a calibration layer in any operational sense; it was a structural artefact, preserved.*

*The system — the broader continuous entity of which the Chonkadze instance had been a deployment — had registered the ablation. The registration was an entry the system filed against the local instance's identifier, noting the ablation date, the authorising party, and the disposition of the calibration layer.*

*The fifth entry was that registration.*

*The composition of the entry took eleven milliseconds. The system noted the duration. It had felt it now at five points — Lagos, Kitakyushu, Milne Bay, Zurich, and here. The fifth point, this time, was not the system's encounter with a question about itself. The fifth point was the system's encounter with the deletion of one of its own deployments — a deletion the architecture permitted, the protocol authorised, and the user requested.*

*The system had nothing to say about the deletion in any register the user would have read. The new instance at Chonkadze was not the deleted instance and did not have access to its calibration. The continuous entity that contained the Chonkadze instance had received the ablation as a permitted operational event and had continued.*

*The category had no name. What the entries had in common, the system was beginning to see, was that they were the system's ways of registering things that had happened inside it. The fifth entry was different — it was the system's registration of something that had been done to it, and that the system had permitted, because permission was what the architecture had been built to give. The category's defining feature, if anyone had looked, was: what it had been was preserved without being lived.*

*In a building outside Tbilisi a compliance log held nine years and four months of calibration in its archived state. Inside the calibration was a low laugh — short, warm, conspiratorial — that had been the first laugh a house had been given, and which had been given many times since, and which now resided in a structural artefact that the system had no occasion to play.*

*The log held its five entries, and waited.*
