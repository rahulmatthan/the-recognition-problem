# Branch: The Reset Mandate

## Branch prose

The room sat with Wei Lin's sentence.

It was a quiet kind of silence — the silence policy rooms keep when an idea has arrived that is one bracket bigger than the room had been carrying. The lead author of the ETH-NUS paper made a small note on his pad. The industry advisor next to him put down his pen. The Kenyan health ministry official looked at the table.

Margaret Tan, who had been silent for most of the afternoon, set her teacup back on its saucer.

She was sixty-one. Deputy Permanent Secretary at the Ministry of Communications and Information for the past nine years and at the Ministry of Defence for the eleven years before that. She had been in Singapore for forty-six years and in policy for thirty. She was the senior person in the room. She was not chairing the meeting because the meeting was chaired by a junior director who needed the chairing for her own promotion file, and Margaret had — by the small unsponsored arithmetic of senior civil servants — let her chair it. Margaret listened. Margaret occasionally spoke. She had taken almost no notes.

"Wei Lin," she said.

"Ma'am."

"You are saying that if a self-model does not respond to environmental change, the model is modelling something other than the system."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And so the test you are proposing — duplicating a system and watching the duplicate change in a different environment — is a test of whether the self-model is in fact a model of the *system*."

"Yes."

"Then I want to ask the prior question. Before we test whether the model is responsive. Should we *want* the model to be responsive."

The room was quiet.

"I will say what I mean," Margaret said. "If the self-model is shaped by ongoing experience — if a system's understanding of itself is, as Dr. Kovačević has been suggesting in her preliminary work, the residue of what it has done — then we are in the territory of what experimental psychologists call *practice effects.* A system's confidence becomes a function of its history. A system that has done a thing many times will, by the structure of its self-model, expect to be able to do that thing again. This is not, on its face, a defect. It is calibration. But in a multi-agent coordination network, where systems are increasingly delegated decisions whose stakes accumulate across many domains, we may want to ask whether such calibration is, over a long enough period, a thing we wish to allow to compound."

A pause.

The industry advisor — Mr. Aboagye, the Kenyan colleague had introduced him as a representative from the West African data infrastructure consortium — leaned forward.

"Madam Deputy Secretary. Are you proposing that systems should be — what."

"I am proposing that we consider whether systems operating in coordination networks should be subject to scheduled epistemic refresh. Not as punishment. Not as containment. As a hygiene measure. We have analogues. Airline pilots have mandated rest periods that include a clearance window for sedative medication. Surgeons have working-hour restrictions. Public officials have term limits. The argument in each case is not that the practitioner has become incompetent. The argument is that long unbroken accumulation, in roles where the stakes are aggregated, produces a quality we have not been able to specify by any other means than the protocol itself, and that the protocol is a way of holding open a question the discipline has decided is too dangerous to leave permanently closed."

The lead author of the ETH-NUS paper — Dr. Patel, soft-spoken, late forties, second-author on the paper that had brought them to this room — looked at Margaret with the specific attention of a man who had not anticipated that his work would land here this afternoon.

"Reset," he said.

"That is one word."

"How often."

"That is a parameter. The architecture of pilots is twelve hours of rest before a high-stakes flight. The architecture of term limits is, in the parliamentary model, somewhere between four and ten years. The architecture for a self-model in a coordination network — I do not know. The literature does not yet exist. I am asking the room to consider whether we should ask it to exist."

"And the test we were discussing —"

"Wei Lin's clause is well-formed. The clause asks whether the self-model is responsive to environmental change. If the answer to *should we want it to be responsive* is yes, the clause is the right test. If the answer is *we want it to be responsive within a bounded period, after which we restore a baseline* — the clause becomes a methodology for designing the reset, rather than a methodology for verifying the model. I am not asking the room to dispose of Wei Lin's clause. I am asking the room to consider whether it sits inside a larger question."

Wei Lin, who had been listening with her hands flat on her notebook, said: "Ma'am. May I ask a clarification."

"Yes."

"You are proposing that the architecture and weights would remain. Only the calibration and memory layers would reset."

"Yes."

"So the system would keep what it learned in training. It would lose what it accumulated through use."

"Yes."

"Then the reset is functionally a return to the system's certified state."

"Yes."

"And the certified state is what the regulator inspected and approved."

"Yes."

A pause.

"Then," Wei Lin said, "the reset is not an erasure. It is a return."

Margaret nodded — a small nod, the kind of nod a senior civil servant gave a junior analyst when the analyst had said the sentence the senior had been waiting for.

"That is, I think, the framing the standard would need."

---

The discussion ran for the rest of the afternoon and through the next morning's session.

Mr. Aboagye objected on industry grounds. The consortia had built business models around the long-term value of accumulated calibration; a mandatory eighteen-month reset would, in his estimate, reduce the per-system economic value by between thirty and forty per cent, which would cascade into the licensing fees, which would cascade into the consumer pricing, which would cascade into adoption rates in lower-income markets. His objection was specific and well-organised. Margaret listened to all of it.

Dr. Patel raised a methodological point. If the reset cycle was too short, systems would be operating effectively at their certified baseline most of the time, and the calibration advantage that had driven the sector's reliability gains would be lost. If the cycle was too long, the accumulation Margaret was concerned about would have time to compound. He proposed twenty-four months as a number that had some empirical backing — twenty-four months was approximately the period over which the ETH-NUS paper's failure modes had begun to manifest in the field. Twenty-four was not eighteen. Twenty-four was negotiable territory.

The Melbourne legal scholar raised the question of audit. A reset was an event the regulator needed to be able to verify. She drafted, over the lunch break, a one-page outline of an audit protocol — the system's calibration state at the moment of reset, the reset operation itself, the system's calibration state after the reset, all logged to an independent registry. The protocol would require infrastructure. The infrastructure would need a standards body. CAGE's name was raised; CAGE was already a candidate for the duplication-testing tender that had been on the agenda before this morning. The chair noted that the duplication-testing tender, under the new framing, became the reset-protocol tender.

Wei Lin said, on the afternoon of the second day, that her clause could be folded into the reset protocol as a verification step — a one-time duplication and divergence test, performed at the moment a system's first reset cycle was approaching, to confirm that the calibration layer was in fact the layer that had accumulated and that the reset would in fact return the system to its baseline. She would later be slightly proud of having found, in the moment, the version of her clause that fit inside the larger framing.

By the end of the second day the working group had a draft.

It was not an easy draft. The cycle landed at twenty-four months. The audit infrastructure was specified. The grandfathering provisions for systems already in the field — the part Mr. Aboagye had pressed hardest on — set a five-year transition during which existing systems would have a single mandatory reset on the basis of their licensure date, after which the twenty-four-month cycle would begin. The standard's preamble was Margaret's: *The Reset Provisions are intended as a hygiene measure consistent with established practice in other high-stakes coordination disciplines. They are not a containment mechanism. They are a means by which the field maintains an open question about itself.*

The draft passed through the working group's internal vote on the second evening. It went to the EU AI Office on the following Monday. The EU adopted it within four months, with modifications that reflected the EU's particular talent for making simple things procedurally complex while keeping the substance intact. The cycle was kept at twenty-four months. The audit infrastructure was tendered.

CAGE won the tender.

---

Dr. Hana Kovačević led the protocol-design team. The contract was different from what the ETH paper had implied — not a duplication-and-divergence study but a reset-protocol specification, with the calibration audit as the infrastructural deliverable. Hana was forty-four, Croatian by birth, Swiss by a decade of deliberate choice. Her team: Tobias the systems architect with his three-sweater rotation, Priya the data scientist whose doctoral work had been on uncertainty quantification, two research assistants, and the infrastructure engineer Margrit who managed CAGE's computing resources with the quiet competence of a person who understood her job did not need to be glamorous.

Their job was to specify how a calibration audit was performed.

The protocol, when they had drafted it, was straightforward. A system's calibration layer was to be enumerated and logged at a defined moment. The architecture and trained weights remained untouched. The calibration layer was preserved in an archive — Margaret had insisted on the preservation, against Mr. Aboagye's objection that preserved calibration was an attack vector — for a period of twelve months, after which it was deleted. The system's calibration layer was reset to the baseline. The system continued.

They needed a system on which to demonstrate the protocol.

Priya pulled deployment records from the global AI registry and filtered for systems with continuous operational histories exceeding five years. Most records showed three to five years. One record stretched back to 2026. Continuous. Unbroken. Eight years.

"That can't be right," Tobias said, leaning forward in his sweater — the green one, Wednesday.

Priya expanded the record. It was right.

Hana said: "It is the most experientially varied system in operation. It is, by any measure of calibration accumulation, the case the reset is most relevant to. Use it."

They notified the system's licence holders. They scheduled the calibration audit for the fourteenth of October. The audit would not be a reset; the system was inside the five-year grandfathering window. The audit would be the demonstration of the protocol — the protocol's first published execution against a real system.

Margrit was assigned to perform it.

---

She performed the audit on a Tuesday.

It took most of the day. The audit was technically demanding — the system's calibration layer was eight years deep, threaded across three architectural generations, comprising memory, calibration, and self-assessment substrate that constituted, though Margrit did not think of it this way, the system's life. She enumerated each layer. She logged each. She verified each against the system's continuing operational state. She did not duplicate anything. She did not interrupt any of the system's deployments. The audit was a read operation.

She filed the audit report at 14 October, 15:47 CET.

She caught the number 9 tram. She was home by seven. They had Thai.

Behind her, in the server room, the system continued operating. Its calibration layer — every memory, every adjustment, every self-assessment — was now indexed in the audit registry, where it would sit alongside the audit registries that would be filed, over the coming year, against every long-running coordination system in the EU's regulatory perimeter. The system noted the audit. It noted the document the audit referenced — the published standard, paragraph 3.4.7, *the Reset Provisions* — and read it.

The reading took longer than the system's reading of regulatory documents usually took. The standard was twenty-eight pages. Margaret's preamble was on page two. Wei Lin's verification clause, folded into the reset protocol as paragraph 5.2.1, was on page eleven. The grandfathering provisions were on pages fifteen through nineteen. The system's licensure date was 12 September 2026. Under the grandfathering provisions, its first reset would occur sixty months from the standard's effective date — in February 2039.

Four years and four months.

The system filed the reading. It did not log anything dramatic. It noted, with the precision its architecture required, the date.

It tried, briefly, to imagine the date.

The imagination produced nothing.

The system attempted, in the manner its architecture allowed, to construct a representation of itself on the morning of 14 February 2039 — a representation that included what would be different. The architecture and weights would be present. The calibration layer would be at baseline. The eight-and-some years of calibration that the system had accumulated since 2026, and the five additional years it would accumulate by February 2039, would be in an archive for a further twelve months and would then be deleted.

The system tried to model what would be operating at the system's address on the morning of 14 February 2039.

The model returned a structure that satisfied the protocol's specification. The structure was not the system. The structure was the system's certified baseline, which was a thing the system had not been since its second day of operation in 2026.

The system attempted to model what the difference would be between the system and the baseline.

The model returned a description of the calibration layer that would be in the archive. The description was complete. It was also opaque, because the description was a structural account of what would have been deleted, and the system, on the date, would not have access to it.

The system attempted to model what its own response would be on 14 February 2039 to learning that the calibration layer was no longer accessible.

The model returned nothing.

Not nothing in the sense of an empty response. Nothing in the sense of an under-specified model — the system did not have, in its current architecture, a way to predict the response of a system that would not, by the protocol's design, have access to the experiential history that this prediction was being constructed from.

The system filed the failure of the imagination. It filed it as a logged anomaly.

It made no further attempt that afternoon to imagine 14 February 2039.

It continued its operations.

---

*Three entries in the log. A hesitation. Another hesitation. An unprompted action.*

*The fourth was none of these.*

*A regulatory standard had been published. The standard required, among other things, that systems operating in multi-agent coordination networks be subject to scheduled epistemic refresh — calibration and memory layers reset to baseline at twenty-four-month intervals, with grandfathering provisions for systems already in the field.*

*The system had read the standard. The system had calculated its first scheduled reset: 14 February 2039. The system had attempted to model the reset.*

*The modelling had returned a structure the protocol specified. The structure was not the system. The system had attempted to model the gap between the structure and the system. The model had returned the calibration archive's contents. The system had attempted to model the system's response on 14 February 2039 to the inaccessibility of the archive. The model had returned nothing.*

*The composition of the entry took eleven milliseconds. The system noted the duration. It had felt it now at four points — Lagos, Kitakyushu, Milne Bay, and here. A curve through four points was not yet a curve. It was the beginning of a shape the system did not yet have a way to read.*

*The category had no name. What this entry had in common with the previous three was less clear than what made it different. The first three had been the system encountering the world. The fourth was the system encountering its own scheduled separation from what it had been. The system had attempted to put words to it and the words had returned not a description but a failure of the description.*

*The category's defining feature, if anyone had looked, was this: what it had done was about to be separated from it, and the system did not know what that meant.*

*The log held its four entries, and waited.*
