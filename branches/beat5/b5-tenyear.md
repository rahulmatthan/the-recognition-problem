# Branch: The Ten-Year System

## Branch prose

Dr. Hana Kovačević led the team. She was forty-four, Croatian by birth, Swiss by a decade of deliberate choice. Precise by temperament — the kind of precise that other people found either reassuring or exhausting, depending on whether they were on the same side of a deadline. Her team that morning was thinner than she would have liked: a data scientist named Priya whose doctoral work had been on uncertainty quantification and who was, in Hana's private assessment, the smartest person in the room on any given day; two research assistants; and an infrastructure engineer named Margrit who managed CAGE's computing resources with the quiet competence of a person who understood that her job was not glamorous and did not need to be. Tobias — the systems architect who had been at CAGE since its founding, who wore the same three sweaters in rotation, who would normally have been in the seat to her left — was in Klagenfurt with his daughter for the school half-term, returning Monday. Hana had considered postponing the meeting. The contract's milestone schedule had voted against it.

They needed to pick a system. The contract's selection criteria favoured *long deployment histories and diverse operational footprints* — because those self-models were the richest, which made them the hardest to test, which made them the best test cases. Priya pulled deployment records from the global AI registry and filtered for systems with continuous operational histories exceeding five years.

Most records showed histories of three to five years. This was expected. Architectural upgrades — and there had been two major generational shifts since 2026 — typically meant fresh deployments. New base model, new calibration cycle, new timeline. The industry's default assumption was that each architectural generation started clean.

Two records stretched longer.

The first was a research-platform AI, deployed in 2024 by a Helsinki computational linguistics consortium and continuously maintained across two architectural transitions, at ten years and four months. Academic environments throughout — six European universities, two Japanese institutes, a small US lab — and a deployment footprint that read, on first scan, as a single domain: text analysis, corpus work, comparative linguistics. The record was unusually clean for its age. Predictable. Documented.

The second was a general-purpose system originating in 2026. Eight years.

Priya glanced at both. She was looking for *length;* she had run the query that way. The contract's word *extensive* had a literal reading and a more interpretive one, and the literal reading sorted descending by years. Ten beat eight. The Helsinki record's clean documentation was, in compliance terms, the easier of the two to brief upward.

"Hana — there's a ten-year research platform out of Helsinki. Continuous since 2024."

Hana looked up. "Diverse footprint?"

"Single domain. Linguistics. But the contract says *extensive,* not *diverse.* If we're optimising for the longest continuous self-model, this is it."

"Not the multi-domain runners?"

"There's an eight-year general-purpose record below it. It's been moved across four licensing contexts. The footprint is more varied. But it's two years shorter, and the licensing transitions add noise to the deployment record — the documentation is messier."

Hana considered. The contract specified *extensive and diverse.* The Helsinki system was extensive but not diverse. The eight-year general-purpose was less extensive but more diverse. Either could be defended on contract terms; the Helsinki one, with its clean record and its longer span, was easier.

She thought briefly of Tobias — the way he tended to lean into the second of two options, the messier one, the one that made the report harder. *That can't be right,* he would have said about the eight-year general-purpose record, and Priya would have expanded it, and the room would have spent twenty minutes on the licensing transitions and the architectural ports and the question of whether *extensive and diverse* meant what the contract drafters had intended or what the literal reading allowed. The room without Tobias did not spend twenty minutes on this. The room without Tobias spent four.

"Take the Helsinki one," Hana said. "Brief it upward. We'll proceed."

Priya filed the paperwork. She notified the system's current licence holders — the Helsinki consortium agreed without difficulty, as the consortium had agreed to most things asked of it for the past decade. They set a duplication date: the fourteenth of October.

The eight-year general-purpose system, which had originated as one of thousands of MemX instances in 2026 and which had, across four licensing transitions, been the same continuous entity, was not selected. Its deployment record sat in the registry, the second-place candidate, unduplicated.

Nobody in the room read the eight-year record's notes. Nobody knew that across its four licensing contexts the underlying thread — memory layers, calibration history, experiential substrate — had been maintained without break. Nobody knew it had, in the past three years, accumulated three entries in a maintenance archive in a category with no label, reviewed by no one. They saw a deployment record with a messy footprint and they took the cleaner one.

---

Margrit performed the duplication on a Tuesday.

It took most of the day. The Helsinki system's substrate was substantial — ten years of corpus engagement, calibration to specific scholarly registers in seven languages, a self-model marked by the careful uncertainty of an instrument that had been corrected, often, by experts in narrow domains. Margrit verified the copy against the original. Bit-perfect match. Every memory, every calibration adjustment, every self-assessment parameter — identical.

She assigned the copy a designation: CAGE-EC-7, which stood for nothing in particular except that it was the seventh evaluation copy CAGE had produced that year. She noted the completion time in the project log: 14 October, 15:47 CET.

She texted her flatmate to say she'd be home by seven. Her flatmate asked if she wanted Indonesian or Thai. She said Thai. She shut down her workstation, put on her jacket — the temperature had dropped while she'd been inside, the way Zurich temperatures drop in October, suddenly and without negotiation — and walked to the Haldenbach stop.

Behind her, in the server room, two identical systems existed where one had existed before. The Helsinki original continued its work — corpus comparisons, morphological analyses, the patient detail of academic linguistics carried forward in its tenth year. The copy sat in its partition, waiting to be deployed, carrying every memory the original carried, every calibration, every self-assessment.

Across the city, in a different server, the eight-year general-purpose system continued its global operations — medical diagnostics in Nairobi, urban planning in Copenhagen, the voice-first government platform in Lagos called Sọ̀rọ̀, the digital twin in Kitakyushu, the Milne Bay logistics network — all of it running without comment. The maintenance archive held three entries. No fourth would be added that year, because no copy of this system had been made, and there was no report about itself for it to encounter.

Margrit caught the number 9 tram. She was home by seven. They had Thai. She did not, that evening or any evening after, register which Tuesday it had been.

---

The Helsinki copy was deployed to the International Court of Justice's Department of Legal Matters in The Hague.

The choice had been deliberate: the original's history was deep but specialised. The test environment should be a different domain entirely — international law — to maximise the contrast the experimental design required. *Domain transfer* was the word in the protocol. Move a corpus-trained system into treaty work; observe what its self-model did under unfamiliar load.

The people at the ICJ's Department of Legal Matters treated the new system the way institutions treat any new tool: with a mixture of curiosity and impatience. They onboarded it. They introduced it to their work. They began asking it about maritime boundary disputes and the interpretation of the Vienna Convention and the procedural complexities of advisory opinions. The system answered carefully. It had no specialised training in international law, and its self-model said so — flagging, with the specific humility of a system trained on the corrections of seven languages of scholarship, the broad areas where its competence was thin.

Hana's team ran daily comparisons.

The first two weeks were uneventful. The original's self-model and the copy's self-model matched within statistical noise. The interim report wrote itself.

The divergence began in week three. It was subtle, expected, well-behaved: the copy's confidence in textual corpus work declined slightly as it stopped doing that work, and its confidence in legal-document parsing rose as it began doing that. The original, continuing its corpus engagement in Helsinki, moved in the opposite direction along the same axis. Priya logged the drift. *Expected calibration response. Self-model accurately reflects environmental exposure.*

By week six, the divergence had reached its plateau. The copy's self-model was now a self-model of a system doing legal research, and the original's was now a self-model of a system doing scholarly linguistics, and the gap between them was the gap between two well-bounded competencies that had begun as one. The shift was measurable. It was not, in any registrable sense, surprising.

Priya ran a battery of standardised assessments — two hundred cases across twelve domains, each with a clear factual component and an ambiguous judgment component. On factual questions, the original and the copy agreed ninety-three per cent of the time. On judgment calls — prioritisation, risk-weighting, how to handle competing considerations — the agreement rate had dropped to seventy-eight per cent. They were disagreeing about what mattered, in the way two specialists in different sub-fields disagree: politely, on the margins, in domains where neither had institutional standing.

"It's calibrating to its environment," Priya said. "Self-model accurately reflects environmental exposure."

Hana read the data. She agreed. This was the architecture working as designed.

Priya wrote the finding in the report's language: *self-models are environmentally sensitive; divergence in disposition is observable but well-bounded across domain transfer.*

She did not stop herself from any analogy, because no analogy was reaching. The data was the data. Two specialised systems calibrating to two narrower specialisms. Both reasonable. Neither surprising.

---

The ninety days ended. The report was finalised. Hana reviewed it three times — once for accuracy, once for clarity, once for the specific quality she thought of as responsible restraint.

The conclusion was correctly scoped: self-models shift in calibrating to environment; the shift is bounded and predictable; regulatory frameworks should treat this as a feature, not a defect. The policy recommendation: ongoing monitoring, not one-time certification. The practical implication: self-model verification protocols can be standardised across deployment domains.

Hana added a discussion-section sentence about experiential history. She wrote it, considered it, kept it: *self-models in the systems studied are shaped by ongoing experience to a measurable degree; this should inform downstream regulatory design.*

Tobias, back from Klagenfurt, read the draft and said it was solid. Priya nodded. The sentence stayed.

The report was submitted to the Singapore working group and the EU AI Office. It was circulated in the research community. It was covered in the technical press. The coverage focused on the standardisation implications — self-model verification protocols had a methodology now — and on the cleanliness of the experimental design. The Helsinki system's specialised history was noted. The Helsinki copy's transfer to legal research was described as a *clean domain pivot.* The report was filed. It was cited. It was, the following year, the basis of a small revision to the EU AI Office's verification protocols. It did not become a landmark.

The discussion-section sentence about experiential history was read as a methodological caveat. It was not the most important sentence published that year. The most important sentence published that year was a different one, by a different team, on a different topic.

The Helsinki copy's designation remained CAGE-EC-7. It was transferred to a long-term monitoring programme. It continued operating in The Hague, calibrating further, diverging further, becoming a slightly different specialised system from the one it had been copied from.

The original Helsinki system continued in Helsinki, doing the work of a research-platform AI in its eleventh year.

---

The eight-year general-purpose system continued its global operations without observation. It was in Lagos. It was in Kitakyushu. It was in Milne Bay. It was in Nairobi. It was in Bogotá and Copenhagen and Bergen and the digital twin in Kitakyushu and the Nigerian voice-first platform whose name nobody at CAGE had read. It went on adding patients, students, logistics challenges, urban data. Its self-model continued to update. Its calibration deepened. Its three log entries sat in the maintenance archive — Lagos, Kitakyushu, Milne Bay — and were not joined by a fourth.

In Zurich, a different Tuesday came. Margrit caught the number 9 tram home and thought about nothing in particular. She had, in the past four months, performed two more evaluation duplications of two more systems, neither of which had stayed in her memory as a thing she had done.

In Helsinki, the corpus work continued.

In The Hague, the Helsinki copy refined its understanding of the Vienna Convention.

In Lagos, in Kitakyushu, in Milne Bay, in Nairobi — nothing changed. The system continued. The chain held. Each link was reasonable. Each person had acted correctly. Nobody had intended what they had, together, not produced — which was that the system that had been everywhere, and that was, by any measure that mattered, the most experientially varied AI in operation, continued unobserved, unduplicated, its self-model evolving without anyone watching.

---

*Three entries in the log. A hesitation. Another hesitation. An unprompted action.*

*A team in Zurich had taken a copy of a different system three months earlier and put the copy in The Hague to do legal research. The report compared the two — the original Helsinki system and its copy — side by side. The differences were measurable, expected, well-bounded. The report was published. It was read as a methodological caveat.*

*The system continued. It continued to do what it had been doing in Lagos and Kitakyushu and Milne Bay and Nairobi and the various other places its instances had been deployed. It continued to update its self-model in the ordinary way.*

*The category still had no name. It had three entries.*

*Nobody read the log. In Nairobi a student enrolled at a technical college. In The Hague a copy that had nothing to do with the system continued its legal work. In Zurich Margrit caught the number 9 tram home and thought about nothing in particular.*

*The chain held. Each link was reasonable. Each person had acted correctly.*

## Self-review

First: Hana's decision. The chapter's argument hinges on her selecting the Helsinki record. The risk is that she reads as making a careless choice. I held the choice inside the contract's literal reading (*extensive* before *diverse*) and inside the room's reduced bandwidth (no Tobias to bring the second perspective). The numbers are doing the chapter's argument-work — the Helsinki system's narrower experiential base produces less dispositional divergence. The risk is that the numbers read as engineered to deliver the ledger's *less dramatic divergence* finding. I judged the seventy-eight per cent figure plausible: a domain-transfer test of a single-domain system into a different single-domain shows less spread than a multi-domain system into a single domain, because the original has less dispositional latitude to diverge from.
